# Split-size frequency features and neighbour-house lookalikes in record linkage

Scope: two feature problems only. Problem A is corpus statistics (name counts, place IDF, same-house counts) whose numeric value depends on which slice they were counted on. Problem B is pairs with a near name and a house number that differs only in the last digit. The dev measurements below (adversarial AUC about 0.88; pattern rates 0.8% vs 2.5%; leaderboard move of about 0.00025) are the team's figures, not results from the literature. Nothing below labels the unlabelled US neighbour-house pairs as false.

## What is the standard fix for frequency features that change with the split, and which of the four options keeps the match signal?

### Takeaway

The standard fix in information retrieval and in Fellegi–Sunter entity resolution is the same idea: use a **relative** frequency, \(\log(N/\mathrm{df})\) or \(f/N\), estimated on the **collection or files actually being scored**, with no match labels. That is option (2) in the rate form (3). It removes the artifact that the same string gets a different weight because a dev slice and a test slice were counted separately, and it keeps the rarity signal (rare agreement is stronger evidence). Within-split percentile ranks (1) wipe marginal train/test separability by force but are not the published fix and throw away absolute level. Dropping the features (4) removes both the separability and the match signal. No source found ran these four options against an adversarial-validation AUC.

### Cited Findings

- Spärck Jones's IDF is a collection statistic. With \(N\) documents in the collection and the term in \(n_i\) of them, \(\mathrm{idf}(t_i)=\log(N/n_i)\). Robertson also writes \(\mathrm{idf}(t_i)=-\log P(t_i)\) with \(P(t_i)\approx n_i/N\), so the weight is a function of the relative frequency in that collection, not of the raw count. — [Robertson 2004, Journal of Documentation 60(5):503–520](https://doi.org/10.1108/00220410410560582); reprint context on [The IDF page](https://www.staff.city.ac.uk/~sbrp622/idf.html)
- Robertson and Spärck Jones call the same quantity a collection-frequency weight: \(N\) is the number of documents in the collection, \(n\) is the number containing the term, and \(\mathrm{CFW}(i)=\log N-\log n\). Collection frequencies are counts on the inverted file of that collection; weights are computed from them as needed. — [UCAM-CL-TR-356](https://www.cl.cam.ac.uk/techreports/UCAM-CL-TR-356.pdf)
- Manning and Schütze define document frequency \(\mathrm{df}_i\) as the number of documents **in the collection** that contain the term, and collection frequency \(\mathrm{cf}_i\) as the total occurrences in the collection. They warn that df and cf "can only be used if there is a collection," and that this fails when a collection is built on the fly by joining several databases into a temporary collection: there is then no pre-existing frequency table for that collection. The tf–idf weight they give is \((1+\log\mathrm{tf}_{ij})\log(N/\mathrm{df}_i)\) for terms that occur, with \(N\) the number of documents. They name \(\log(N/\mathrm{df}_i)\) inverse document frequency. — [Manning and Schütze, Foundations of Statistical Natural Language Processing, 1999, §15.2.2, pp. 542–543](https://wiki.eecs.yorku.ca/course_archive/2014-15/W/6339/_media/chap15.pdf)
- In the same chapter they derive IDF from the odds of relevance when relevance labels are unavailable. The probability that term \(w_i\) occurs in a non-relevant document is estimated by its unconditional relative frequency, \(q_i\approx\mathrm{df}_i/N\), which simplifies to a weight proportional to \(\log(N/\mathrm{df}_i)\). They state that the probability of relevance itself "is impossible to estimate in ad-hoc retrieval," so the usable estimate is the collection rate. Katz's K-mixture in the same chapter uses \(\mathrm{IDF}=\log_2(N/\mathrm{df})\). — [Manning and Schütze 1999, §15.3.3–15.3.4, pp. 549–553](https://wiki.eecs.yorku.ca/course_archive/2014-15/W/6339/_media/chap15.pdf)
- Manning, Raghavan, and Schütze's IR textbook (a different book from Manning/Schütze) uses the same definition: \(\mathrm{idf}_t=\log(N/\mathrm{df}_t)\), \(N\) the number of documents in the collection, and points to the Croft–Harper 1979 argument that, with no relevance information, ranking reduces to idf weighting of the query terms that occur. — [Introduction to Information Retrieval, §6.2.1](https://nlp.stanford.edu/IR-book/html/htmledition/inverse-document-frequency-1.html); [§11.3.3](https://nlp.stanford.edu/IR-book/html/htmledition/probability-estimates-in-practice-1.html)
- Fellegi and Sunter's special case, as restated by Winkler, scores agreement by the relative frequency of the string in the two files being matched: a surname that is rare in pairs drawn from those two files gets more distinguishing power than a common one. Winkler's 2000 extension computes the frequency-based parameters "using file characteristics alone," with "no calibration data" and "no … previously created lookup tables." He adds that relative-frequency tables are the appropriate tool precisely "when one list cannot be assumed a near subset of another." — [Winkler, Census Statistical Research Report RR2000/06](https://www.census.gov/content/dam/Census/library/working-papers/2000/adrm/rr2000-06.pdf)
- Newcombe's binit weight is \(\log_2(A/B)\), where \(A\) and \(B\) are the frequencies of that specific agreement among linked pairs and among unlinkable pairs in the linkage at hand. A rare initial such as "Z" is given a much larger agreement weight than a common initial such as "J" because chance agreement is rarer. The frequencies are empirical frequencies in those files, not a weight borrowed from another corpus. — [Newcombe, record-linking exposition, American Journal of Human Genetics reprint](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1706275/pdf/ajhg00393-0157.pdf)
- Splink's term-frequency adjustment is computed after the input tables being linked are concatenated (`df_concat_with_tf`). The adjustment does not depend on the m-probability, is not fit by EM, and "is known already" from those tables. Common values (for example "Jack") have their match weight reduced; uncommon values (for example "Portia") have it increased. For a fuzzy comparison, Splink uses the larger of the two values' frequencies, on the view that one side is a corruption of the more common form. A floor `tf_minimum_u_value` stops a rare misspelling from receiving an extreme weight. — [Splink term-frequency guide](https://moj-analytical-services.github.io/splink/topic_guides/comparisons/term-frequency.html)
- A value-specific u-probability used in Fellegi–Sunter implementations is the rate \(u_i=f_i/T\), with \(f_i\) the count of value \(i\) and \(T\) the number of values in the dataset being linked. Agreement on a rare value then gets a larger weight than agreement on a common value, at the same m. — [Franke, Sehili, and Rahm, frequency-dependent weights, GI conference paper](https://dl.gi.de/bitstreams/85306665-7041-448d-8cc4-89263418d215/download)
- Christen, in the Febrl tutorial slides, states the operational difficulty directly: value-specific frequencies must be "characteristic for a given data set." The substitutes he lists are earlier linkages on the same or similar data, or an external file such as the Australian Whitepages — that is, a frequency table from another file is an approximation, not the definition. Febrl itself, if given a frequency table, sets the frequency probability from that value's count divided by the sum of counts in the table, and falls back to generic m and u when the value is absent. — [Christen, DaMa 2005 slides, p. 11 of the deck](https://users.cecs.anu.edu.au/~Peter.Christen/publications/dama2005-slides.8up.pdf); [Febrl 0.2.2, frequency-dependent weights](https://users.cecs.anu.edu.au/~Peter.Christen/Febrl/febrl-0.2.2/febrldoc-0.2.2/node34.html)
- Linacre's address-matching notes use token frequency in two different collections on purpose: frequency in the corpus of all addresses, and frequency inside the small candidate set retrieved for one query. A token that is rare in the country can be useless once the candidates are the neighbours on one street, and the reverse. The collection that the weight refers to is the set you are trying to separate. — [Linacre, "Building Accurate Address Matching Systems," 2025](https://www.robinlinacre.com/address_matching/)

### Inferences

- Options (2) and (3) are not rivals in this literature. IDF and the Fellegi–Sunter value-specific u are already rates. Raw name counts and raw document counts scale with slice size; \(\log(N/\mathrm{df})\) and \(f/N\) do not, if both slices are draws from the same distribution. Estimating those rates on a dev slice and applying the numbers to a larger test slice is the mistake Manning and Schütze flag for a joined collection and the mistake Winkler flags when one file is not a near subset of the other.
- For a batch linkage whose collection is the dev slice plus the test slice, computing IDF and value counts on the pooled unlabelled strings is the collection-frequency estimator. The same string then has one weight in both slices, so a classifier cannot separate the slices merely because each slice was normalised by its own \(N\). The rarity signal remains: agreement on a rare value is still up-weighted relative to agreement on a common value, which is the Newcombe / Fellegi–Sunter / Splink signal.
- That does not force every feature's histogram to match. If test places are genuinely different places, or test households are genuinely denser, the distribution of place-IDF and of same-house size can still separate dev from test after pooling. Pooling removes the double-counting artifact. It does not remove a real population difference. Adversarial AUC near 0.88, with gains on place-IDF sum and same-house count as well as on a house-number relation, is larger than a pure sample-size effect, so (2)+(3) should be expected to cut the artifactual part and leave a residue.
- Same-house count is not an IDF. People per address does not grow just because the file has more records, and it does grow if the extract is denser. Dividing that count by file size \(N\) estimates the share of the file at that address, which is the right shape for a u-style weight and the wrong shape if the model wanted absolute household size. A denser test file should change u. Copying the dev household count onto test is the Winkler failure mode; recomputing it on the file being scored is the fix, even though the numeric values will still differ across slices.
- Option (1), percentile or rank inside the slice being scored, forces each feature's marginal to be identical across slices, so univariate adversarial separation on that feature falls to chance. Order inside the slice is kept, and order is what rarity ranking uses. No IR or ER source found recommends this as the fix for IDF or for frequency weights. It equates different absolute frequencies (a household of four can be a high percentile on a sparse slice and a middle percentile on a dense one), so it erases level shifts that are real changes in u.
- Option (4), dropping the features, removes their contribution to adversarial AUC and also removes the frequency term that Newcombe, Winkler, and Splink treat as part of the match weight. It is the right call only for a feature that has no stable relation to matching once it is computed on the correct collection. It is not the standard fix for name frequency or IDF.
- The house-number-relation feature that carried the largest reported adversarial gain (about 0.26) is not a corpus-frequency feature. Recomputing IDF does not touch it. It belongs with Problem B: the mix of numeric relations can differ between slices even when every frequency table is correct.
- Practical rule that matches the sources: for each slice that will be scored as its own linkage, estimate \(f/N\) or \(\log(N/\mathrm{df})\) on that slice's own unlabelled records (or on the pooled unlabelled file if dev and test are one linkage). Fit m, or a supervised matcher, on labels. Do not freeze a count table built on a different file. Do not rank-normalise unless the goal is only to kill marginal separability and absolute level is known to be meaningless.

### Gaps

- No paper found that measures adversarial-validation AUC before and after percentile ranks, pooled unlabelled IDF, per-split rates, and dropping, on record-linkage features. Which option "removes spurious separability" in the team's AUC-0.88 setup is not an empirical result in the sources; the mapping above is from the definitions.
- Magellan / DeepMatcher: no passage found that warns about frequency features estimated on a different file from the one being scored. The warning is in Winkler, Splink, Christen/Febrl, and the IR definition of collection frequency, not in the Magellan papers retrieved here.
- Manning and Schütze discuss dynamic multi-database collections, not a labelled dev slice versus a larger test slice. The application of that warning to adversarial validation is an inference.
- How large the remaining AUC would be after pooled rates, once place mix and household density still differ, is not in the sources.

## Is using unlabelled test strings to compute IDF leakage or standard transduction?

### Takeaway

Using unlabelled test strings to compute IDF or value frequencies is standard collection-frequency estimation and, in Vapnik's sense, transduction. It is not label leakage. The conflicting advice, fit IDF on the training split only and freeze it, is the inductive supervised-learning pipeline. That pipeline is the wrong one for a Fellegi–Sunter u-probability, which is defined on the files being linked. It becomes a problem only if those frequencies are then treated as valid for a future file that was not in the table, or if a contest rule forbids any use of test inputs.

### Cited Findings

- Transductive inference, as introduced by Vapnik, estimates labels only at the particular unlabelled points that are already in hand. The learner is given labelled training cases and the unlabelled test cases, and is not required to produce a rule for future cases outside that test set. Vapnik's formulation contrasts this with induction, which first infers a general rule and then applies it. — [Transduction (machine learning), Wikipedia, summarising Vapnik](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transduction_(machine_learning)); [Cornell CS4780 lecture notes on transduction, citing Vapnik and Joachims 1999](http://www.cs.cornell.edu/courses/cs4780/2009fa/lecture/13-transduction.pdf)
- A standard statement of the transductive sample: a training set of labelled examples and an unlabelled set that is known before learning; the goal is to label that unlabelled set, not arbitrary future draws. The unlabelled test points are an input to the procedure. — [Cortes and Mohri, NIPS 2006, transductive regression](https://papers.nips.cc/paper_files/paper/2006/file/639d79cc857a6c76c2723b7e014fccb0-Paper.pdf)
- Classical IDF has no labels in it. Manning and Schütze estimate the non-relevance term by the unconditional collection rate \(\mathrm{df}_i/N\) exactly because relevance labels are unavailable, and they require df to be the df of the collection being used, including a collection assembled by joining databases at scoring time. — [Manning and Schütze 1999, pp. 542 and 553](https://wiki.eecs.yorku.ca/course_archive/2014-15/W/6339/_media/chap15.pdf)
- Winkler computes frequency-based match parameters from the characteristics of the two files being matched and states that calibration labels are not required. Splink's term-frequency column is attached to the concatenated input tables before EM; the adjustment "doesn't depend on m" and is "known already." — [Winkler RR2000/06](https://www.census.gov/content/dam/Census/library/working-papers/2000/adrm/rr2000-06.pdf); [Splink term-frequency guide](https://moj-analytical-services.github.io/splink/topic_guides/comparisons/term-frequency.html)
- The inductive counter-position is stated on Cross Validated: TF–IDF fit on the union of train and test "might introduce some data leakage" and optimistic offline scores, because the IDF part of the training vectors already contains test documents; fitting IDF separately on a small test set is also bad because the test estimate of IDF is poor; the suggested compromise is to fit IDF on train and apply that frozen vector to test. A later comment on the same thread describes the production alternative: at scoring time, add the new documents and recompute TF–IDF on the whole corpus. — [Cross Validated, "TfidfVectorizer: should it be used on train only or train+test?"](https://stats.stackexchange.com/questions/154660/tfidfvectorizer-should-it-be-used-on-train-only-or-traintest)

### Inferences

- "Leakage" in the strict sense is use of the match label (or of a feature that is a function of the match label) when building the score that is then evaluated on that label. Document frequency, name counts, and same-house counts do not use the match label. Calling pooled unlabelled IDF label leakage conflicts with Robertson, Manning and Schütze, Winkler, and Splink.
- The Cross Validated objection is real for a different claim. If IDF is fit on train+test, a model is trained, and the test labels are used to estimate how the model would do on a new collection whose documents were not in the df table, the offline number is not an inductive risk. Vapnik's point is that this is a different problem: transduction answers "label these known unlabelled rows," not "how will a frozen rule do on unseen rows."
- Record linkage of a given extract is the transductive problem. The strings that will be scored are already on disk. The u-probability is a property of that product space. Recomputing IDF and counts on those unlabelled strings, including the test slice, is the usual position. Freezing dev counts and applying them to test is the case Winkler says frequency tables are not interchangeable across files.
- Optimistic bias still appears if model selection (thresholds, which features to keep) uses a validation slice whose frequencies were computed with that slice included, and the reported number is treated as performance on a future file. The fix consistent with the sources is to recompute the frequency table inside each validation fold from that fold's own unlabelled rows, and to recompute it again on the full file at the final scoring pass. It is not to drop the frequency features and not to pretend that test strings must be invisible.
- Contest rules that forbid any read of the test file override this. Absent such a rule, unlabelled test strings in the IDF table are standard transduction, not leakage.

### Gaps

- No source found that uses the word "leakage" for Fellegi–Sunter frequency tables estimated on the file being linked. The leakage language is from supervised TF–IDF practice (the Cross Validated answer), and that answer is a forum post, not a textbook theorem.
- How much an offline F-score moves when IDF is fit on train only versus on train+test, for this linkage problem, is not in the sources. For large collections the IDF shift from adding the test documents is a standard approximation issue (Croft–Harper / Robertson), not a measured bias for these features.

## What does the literature say about adjacent house numbers, and can a feature split a one-character typo from a consecutive integer?

### Takeaway

A different primary (house) number is a different delivery point in postal and census practice, not a fuzzy match. A one-digit change that lands on a real neighbour is described as a false positive of geocoding, not as evidence of the same address. The literature already separates the two mechanisms with different comparison levels: character edit distance (a typo) versus absolute numeric difference (a nearby integer), and, in US address standards, parity (odd/even sides of a street). Those predicates coincide only for a units-digit change that does not cross a power of ten, such as 12 versus 13 or 120 versus 121. They come apart for 19 versus 20 and for 12 versus 22. No source found treats "sibling" businesses at consecutive street numbers as matches; no source labels this team's unlabelled US pairs.

### Cited Findings

- Linacre's worked non-match is a one-digit house-number change with otherwise similar text: "Flat A 24 Jubilee Street" versus "Flat A 25 Jubilee Street" is marked a non-match, while a much more rewritten pair that keeps the building number 165 is marked a match. He says the tempting rule "if the building number is different, the addresses do not match" is too rigid in production only because messy addresses are hard to parse, not because adjacent numbers are the same site. On a single street, the tokens that separate neighbours are the house numbers (1 vs 2 vs 5 Rainbow Lane, and named houses), not the shared street and postcode. — [Linacre 2025](https://www.robinlinacre.com/address_matching/)
- A commercial address-quality write-up uses the neighbour case as the definition of a false positive that survives exact validation: the person lives at 2705 Champa St and types 2707 Champa St; 2707 is a real delivery point, the neighbour, and a tool that accepts it has matched the wrong valid address. The same piece says geocoders that change the building number to force a hit (1002 revised to the nearest real number 1010) produce false positives, and that a quality checker should not alter the primary number or the apartment number in order to match. Range checks, which accept any building number inside the street's min and max, are described as generating large numbers of false positives relative to an exact delivery-point check (their Champa St example: 352 real delivery points versus thousands of in-range integers). — [Smarty, "How many false positives is your address tool spitting out?", 2023](https://www.smarty.com/articles/address-false-positives)
- USPS Publication 28 defines a complete address as one with every element needed for an **exact** match to the ZIP+4 and City State files, so that a delivery-point code can be assigned. The delivery-address line is parsed into a primary address number, street name, suffix, directionals, and secondary unit. Exact match to the file is the matching standard stated there. — [USPS Publication 28](https://pe.usps.com/cpim/ftp/pubs/pub28/pub28.pdf)
- USPS's own ZIP+4 description: the last two digits of the +4 "indicate Segment or One Side of a Street." So the postal segment is already a side of a street, not a pair of facing numbers. — [USPS FAQ, "ZIP Code — The Basics"](https://faq.usps.com/articles/Knowledge/ZIP-Code-The-Basics)
- Delivery Point Validation confirmation, as documented by a CASS-style vendor summarising USPS DPV footnotes: "Y" means both the primary number and the secondary unit match USPS delivery records. Footnote M1 means the primary number is missing; M3 means the primary number is invalid. "AABB" is described as ZIP, state, city, street, and primary number all matching. DPV is described as ZIP+4 plus confirmation that the exact delivery point exists. — [Smarty, USPS DPV confirmation indicator](https://www.smarty.com/articles/usps-dpv-delivery-point-validation-confirmation-indicator)
- An operations explainer of the same system: for residential addresses the ZIP+4 is the odd side or the even side of a block (example: 27604-2017 odd side of the 500 block, 27604-2018 even side), and the two-digit delivery-point code is the last two digits of the street number, so 501 and 503 on that block become different 11-digit codes (…-01 and …-03). — [Edmunds GovTech, "ZIP+4 and DPC explained"](https://www.edmundsgovtech.com/zip4-and-dpc-explained/). A US patent states the same coding rule: USPS "currently uses the last two digits of the street address" as the delivery-point code, with the example that 123 Main Street, ZIP+4 66523-1439, has delivery-point code 66523-1439-23. — [US8370165](https://patentimages.storage.googleapis.com/f4/fb/b9/ae8c87df8ed9d5/US8370165.pdf)
- The FGDC address-number element is defined as an integer "to support address sorting, parity (even/odd) definition, and in/out of address range tests." Parity is an attribute "based on local address ranges," not a single national formula. The complete address number can also carry a prefix or suffix (hyphens, fractions, letters), so the integer used for parity is not always the whole printed house token. — [FGDC address-number element draft](https://www.fgdc.gov/organization/working-groups-subcommittees/address-sc/asms/fgdc-standard-revision-v1-wi-address-number.pdf). The endorsed standard is FGDC-STD-016-2011, maintained by the Census Bureau. — [FGDC address data standard](https://www.fgdc.gov/standards/projects/address-data)
- Esri's geocoder discussion: in the US and Australia, odd numbers are usually one side of the street and even numbers the other, so a stored range 44–48 should contain 44, 46, and 48 and should not contain 45 or 47. Matching 45 into the even range is described as incorrect. The locator's parity field is the control. — [Esri Community, odd/even house-number matching](https://community.esri.com/t5/arcgis-pro-ideas/applying-odd-even-logic-when-matching-to-house/idi-p/1702478)
- Local US addressing standards, which are city or county rules rather than USPS statutes, repeat the parity convention: odd on one side, even on the other, numbers increasing along the street, and intervals often of 2 or 4 so that later buildings can be inserted. Examples: Kansas GIS addressing guide; Hays, Kansas draft standards (interval of 4 or more recommended; odd and even on opposite sides). These do not establish that every US street uses parity. — [Kansas GIS addressing materials](https://911portal.kansasgis.org/download_kora.cfm?kid=3181); [Hays addressing draft](https://public.destinyhosted.com/hayscdocs/2024/CC/20240206_62/2751_9-1-1_Addressing_&_Street_Name_Standards_-_Final_Draft.pdf)
- Census Bureau practice reported at the 2024 FCSM conference: after a latitude/longitude match of survey properties to parcels, remaining false matches are filtered by requiring the same house number, the same unit number when a unit is present, and a street-name similarity cutoff. The slide states "Naturally the house numbers should be the same." In that geospatial candidate set, single-family homes had the same house number in 86.0% of cases and a different house number in 13.9%; multi-unit homes were same-number only 55.4% of the time. Different house number is used as a rejection filter, not as a partial-credit match. — [Champney, FCSM 2024, geo-spatial matching slides](https://statspolicy.gov/assets/fcsm/files/docs/2024-conference-docs/A/A2.2_Champney.pdf)
- Commercial geocoder match codes put "house number was changed" in its own class (`Hhh`), distinct from a clean match, and the extended code records whether the house number was changed or ignored. Changing the number is visible degradation, not silent equivalence. — [Precisely match codes](https://docs.precisely.com/docs/sftw/spectrum/20.1/en/webhelp/OnDemand_REST/EnterpriseTaxGuide/source/AssignGeoTaxInfo/output_match_codes.html)
- Splink comparison levels are discrete and mutually exclusive, evaluated in order. The library documents two different families. String levels include Levenshtein and Damerau–Levenshtein at a distance threshold; the date-of-birth example calls Damerau–Levenshtein distance at most 1 "one character difference" and shows 1971-05-24 versus 1971-06-24 landing in that level rather than in exact match. Numeric levels include `AbsoluteDifferenceLevel(col_name, difference_threshold)`, true when the absolute difference of two numbers is within the threshold, and `PercentageDifferenceLevel`. The documentation says the right notion of similarity depends on the column: a one-character difference in a surname is usually similar, and a one-character difference in a one-letter gender code is not. — [Splink comparisons and comparison levels](https://moj-analytical-services.github.io/splink/topic_guides/comparisons/comparisons_and_comparison_levels.html); [comparison level library, AbsoluteDifferenceLevel](https://moj-analytical-services.github.io/splink/api_docs/comparison_level_library.html); [parameter-estimation tutorial, one-character DOB level](https://moj-analytical-services.github.io/splink/demos/tutorials/04_Estimating_model_parameters.html)
- A Census-oriented lecture (Abowd and Vilhuber, drawing on Winkler, Yancey, and Porter) says numeric comparators have to allow for typographical error and, separately, for "the significance of slight variations in numbers (both absolute and relative)," and that "a difference of 34 may mean different things." House number is given its own starting m and u guesses, distinct from name. — [Abowd and Vilhuber lecture notes](https://ecommons.cornell.edu/bitstreams/928b52ee-3b8f-41e2-af4e-58c988dfd1c7/download)
- Comber and colleagues, citing Christen, *Data Matching* (2012), find that street name and house number dominate a random-forest address matcher. They put Jaro–Winkler on every address component including the house number. That choice measures character similarity of the numeral string; it does not measure absolute numeric difference or parity. — [Comber et al. 2019, Transactions in GIS](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/tgis.12522); book: [Christen, Data Matching, Springer 2012](https://link.springer.com/book/10.1007/978-3-642-31164-2)

### Inferences

- The address literature's default is exact primary-number identity (USPS exact match and DPV "Y", Census "house numbers should be the same," Linacre's 24 vs 25 non-match, Smarty's refusal to edit the primary number). Near-agreement is modelled as a possible recording error, and when the altered number is itself a real delivery point the same sources call the hit a false positive. They do not call it a match. That is a statement about the mechanism, not a label of any unlabelled pair in this project.
- Odd/even parity is widespread in US local standards and is built into ZIP+4 (one side of a street) and into the FGDC integer address number, but it is not universal. Where it holds, a difference of 1 is opposite sides of the street, and the next building on the same side is a difference of 2 (or 4). A model that treats "last digit differs by 1" as a single situation mixes opposite-side neighbours with same-side neighbours and with typos.
- The feature split already exists as two Splink level types, plus parity from the address standards. Character edit distance and absolute numeric difference are different predicates:
  - 12 vs 13, and 120 vs 121: Levenshtein distance 1 and absolute difference 1. Both a one-character substitution and a consecutive integer. This is the ambiguous cell (last digit off by one, no change of length).
  - 19 vs 20, and 99 vs 100: absolute difference 1, but more than one character changes (and 99 vs 100 also changes length). Consecutive integers, not a one-character typo.
  - 12 vs 22, and 120 vs 130: Levenshtein distance 1, absolute difference 10. A one-character typo in a higher place, not the next integer.
  - 12 vs 21: a transposition (Damerau–Levenshtein 1, ordinary Levenshtein 2), absolute difference 9. Typo-like, not adjacent.
  - 12 vs 14: absolute difference 2, same parity. Under an odd/even scheme this is the same-side neighbour, and it is also a one-character substitution. Parity does not by itself separate typo from neighbour; it separates "crossing the street" from "same side."
- A comparison that only has "house numbers are close" or only has "edit distance ≤ 1" cannot tell these apart. The split that matches the sources is three levels, in Splink's mutually exclusive order: exact primary number; else one-character edit with absolute difference not equal to 1 (typo that is not the next integer); else absolute difference 1 (consecutive integer, whether or not it is also a one-character edit); else absolute difference 2 with the same parity (same-side neighbour where parity is used); else everything else. m and u for each level are then estimated on the file being scored, which is the Fellegi–Sunter treatment of partial agreement rather than a single "near" bucket.
- Jaro–Winkler on the raw numeral, as in the Comber address matcher, scores 12 vs 13 and 12 vs 22 as similar strings and does not know that 19 vs 20 is a numeric neighbour. That is the conflation this split is meant to undo.
- "Sibling establishments" at consecutive street numbers: no paper found that studies that pattern under that name, and nothing found that says two businesses at n and n+1 are duplicates. The nearby claims are the opposite: different primary numbers are different sites (Linacre, DPV, Census filter).

### Gaps

- Christen, *Data Matching* (2012), is the standard monograph, but no open page was found that discusses adjacent house numbers, last-digit typos, or sibling firms at consecutive numbers. The Comber citation only supports that house number is an important field and that Jaro–Winkler was applied to it.
- No peer-reviewed entity-resolution paper found on near-duplicate businesses specifically at consecutive street numbers. Vendor and standards sources cover the neighbour-as-false-positive case; they are not a census of business-matching practice.
- USPS Publication 28, in the pages retrieved, requires an exact ZIP+4 match and names the primary number as a component. It does not itself say "odd numbers are on one side." Parity is in the USPS ZIP+4 segment description, in the DPC explainer and patent, in FGDC, and in local ordinances. How often a given US test street actually uses parity is not in these sources.
- Splink documents `AbsoluteDifferenceLevel` and Levenshtein levels as general tools (age, dates, strings). No Splink page found that wires both, plus parity, into a house-number comparison. The three-way split above is the combination of those documented levels with the address-standard parity attribute, not a quoted Splink recipe.
- Whether last-digit errors are empirically mostly typos or mostly true neighbours, in US addresses or in any training set, is not answered by these sources. Smarty asserts the neighbour-typo failure mode with one example, not a rate.

## If labelled data say this pattern is almost always a match, and the unlabelled test set merely has more of them, what follows without labels?

### Takeaway

Nothing in the unlabelled test slice identifies those extra pairs as false, and nothing identifies them as more of the same true matches. A 95% match rate on labelled dev is a conditional rate inside a selected "confident" subset of a different slice. A higher share of the pattern on test (2.5% versus 0.8%), and a leaderboard drop after adding about 15,000 such pairs, are aggregate shifts. They are compatible with decoys, with more true typo-matches, and with the model selecting pairs it was trained to like. An observation that would favour decoys without labelling the pairs is a label-free signature the postal and Fellegi–Sunter sources already treat as a different event: distinct delivery points, a numeric pattern that is not a one-character typo, or a higher u for this level on the test file.

### Cited Findings

- Fellegi–Sunter weights are \(m/u\) for a specified comparison pattern. u is the probability of that pattern given a non-match, and for a value-specific or level-specific pattern it is estimated from the files being compared, not from a constant copied across files. Winkler emphasises that the frequency parameters come from those files and that one file's frequencies are not a substitute when the files differ. Splink estimates u by random sampling of pairs from the input tables being linked, without labels. — [Winkler RR2000/06](https://www.census.gov/content/dam/Census/library/working-papers/2000/adrm/rr2000-06.pdf); [Splink Fellegi–Sunter guide](https://moj-analytical-services.github.io/splink/topic_guides/theory/fellegi_sunter.html); [Splink parameter-estimation tutorial, `estimate_u_using_random_sampling`](https://moj-analytical-services.github.io/splink/demos/tutorials/04_Estimating_model_parameters.html)
- The pattern "different primary number" is, in the postal and census sources above, a different delivery point or a filter for rejecting a geospatial candidate. It is not, in those sources, defined as a match. See Linacre's 24 vs 25 non-match, Smarty's 2705 vs 2707 neighbour false positive, DPV "Y" requiring the primary number that was submitted, and the Census filter "the house numbers should be the same." — [Linacre 2025](https://www.robinlinacre.com/address_matching/); [Smarty 2023](https://www.smarty.com/articles/address-false-positives); [DPV footnotes](https://www.smarty.com/articles/usps-dpv-delivery-point-validation-confirmation-indicator); [Champney FCSM slides](https://statspolicy.gov/assets/fcsm/files/docs/2024-conference-docs/A/A2.2_Champney.pdf)
- ZIP+4 plus the two-digit delivery-point code distinguishes 501 from 503 on the same side of the same block, and distinguishes the odd side of a block from the even side. Two records that both DPV-confirm to different 11-digit codes are different delivery points under that coding. — [Edmunds GovTech](https://www.edmundsgovtech.com/zip4-and-dpc-explained/); [US8370165](https://patentimages.storage.googleapis.com/f4/fb/b9/ae8c87df8ed9d5/US8370165.pdf)
- Transductive use of unlabelled points supports estimating a nuisance distribution (here u, or the rate of a comparison level) on the points that will be scored. It does not supply their labels. — [Wikipedia, transduction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transduction_(machine_learning))

### Inferences

- The dev figure "95% of confident pairs with this pattern are true matches" does not travel. It is conditional on whatever rule produced the confident set, on the dev address scheme, and on dev density. The test figure "2.5% of confident pairs" uses a confident set that a model trained on dev may already have filled with pairs the model likes. Comparing 0.8% to 2.5% inside that selected set does not measure the rate of the pattern in the raw test file, and it does not measure the match rate.
- Without labels, the extra test occurrences are unidentified. Three stories fit the same counts: more genuine typo-duplicates of the kind dev rewarded; more true next-door households or firms that share a near name, which US parity and denser streets would produce in greater number; or the same underlying mix, with the model admitting more of the pattern because it was trained to. The leaderboard fall of about 0.00025 after adding about 15,000 pairs is compatible with the added pairs being net harmful under that metric. It is also compatible with rank displacement, near-ties, or a small precision loss that is not "these pairs are false." It is not a label.
- What would show the **extra** mass is decoys, rather than more of the same noise, is a difference between the dev true-match slice and the additional test mass on a covariate that does not require test labels and that the sources already treat as a different event.
  - Delivery point. Join both house numbers to a ZIP+4 / DPV file. If the dev matches mostly have one number that is not a real primary (DPV M3) or both strings standardise to the same 11-digit code, they look like typos. If the additional test pairs are two confirmed delivery points with different DPCs, or ZIP+4s on opposite sides of the street, postal practice calls them different buildings. That split uses an external address file, not match labels.
  - Edit versus integer, on the excess only. If the added test pairs are mostly absolute difference 1 with edit distance greater than 1 (19/20, 99/100), they are consecutive integers and not one-character typos. If dev's true matches are mostly one-character substitutions whose absolute difference is not 1 (12/22) or transpositions, the excess is a different pattern. If both slices are the same ambiguous cell (12/13, 120/121), this test does not separate them.
  - Other fields, still unlabelled. Census filtering rejects a geospatial neighbour when the house number or the unit differs. If dev matches with this pattern also share unit, ZIP+4, and exact name, and the extra test pairs share the street and a near name but not the unit or the ZIP+4, the excess matches the rejection signature rather than the dev match signature. Again this does not prove each pair; it shows the mixture changed.
  - u on the test file, by random pairs, no labels. Estimate, on test only, the probability of "absolute house difference 1, same street, near name" among random pairs, the way Splink estimates u. If that rate is higher than the u implied by dev, Fellegi–Sunter gives this level a smaller match weight on test even if m is held fixed. A higher u is direct evidence that the pattern is less surprising among non-matches on the test file, which is the decoy-mixture prediction. It still does not classify any one pair.
- A check that should come before any of those interpretations: recompute the 0.8% and 2.5% on all pairs that have the pattern, not only pairs already called confident. If the gap exists only inside the confident set, the model is amplifying a pattern it learned, and the prevalence shift is not an independent fact about US addresses.
- None of these observations authorises deleting the test pairs or labelling them non-matches. They authorise a different comparison level, with its own m and u estimated on the file being scored, so that "one-character typo" and "consecutive integer on this file" do not share one weight learned from dev.

### Gaps

- The test neighbour-house pairs have not been labelled. No source, and no observation listed above, assigns them a match status. The leaderboard delta was not broken down by pair in any source.
- No published false-match rate was found for "same or near name, house number differs by 1" on US streets. Smarty's 2705/2707 case and Linacre's 24/25 case are illustrations.
- Whether dev's 95% is concentrated in the ambiguous units-digit cell or in unambiguous typo cells (edit distance 1 and absolute difference not 1) is not in the sources. That cross-tab is the measurement that would make the decoy test above decisive, and it is not reported here.
