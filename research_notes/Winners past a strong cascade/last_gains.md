# Last gains once candidate recall is already ~99.75% (macro F0.5)

Scope note for the report writer: the team’s candidate generator already retrieves about 99.75% of true pairs, so a better embedder is arithmetically capped at roughly a 0.0025 recall gap and they estimate ≤0.001 on the score. Training countries are labelled. One test country (about 15% of entities) has no labels. A dev ranking has been backward versus the public leaderboard twice. Do not turn any finding below into a recommendation to add external data, a geocoder, or self-training on that unlabelled country. None of the papers below report macro F0.5; they report pair F1, cluster purity, precision, and recall. Where two sources disagree, both are quoted.

## What still moves F1 or precision after blocking recall is already above 98%?

### Takeaway
The only 2025–2026 study that measures blocking recall and then ablates the stages after it is Pavani et al. (Walmart, arXiv:2607.26298, 28 Jul 2026). Once blocker recall is already 97.7–100%, more retrieval moves recall by +0.3 to +0.4 percentage points, while precision moves by rules and a sparsity-specific threshold (up to about +32 purity points on one small set, about +8 and +4 on MusicBrainz) and clustering can erase pair F1 entirely if transitive closure is left on. MERAI, Splink, Ditto, AnyMatch, Sudowoodo, Unicorn, and MatchGPT do not publish an ablation that starts from blocking recall already above 98%.

### Cited Findings
- Pavani, Aluri, Jadhav, Prasad, and Sanka, “Entity Resolution in Practice: Lessons from a Self-Serve Pipeline,” arXiv:2607.26298v1, 28 Jul 2026, https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.26298. Six dedup benchmarks (Restaurants 864 records through NCV 5M). Blocker recall at k=20, Table IV: DBLP-Scholar 100.0 / 100.0 / 100.0 (single HNSW / ensemble / ensemble+identifier blocking); MusicBrainz 200K 97.7 / 98.1 / 98.1; NCV 97.7 / 97.8 / 98.1. Prose: a single M=16 index misses 67 true MusicBrainz pairs (97.7% recall); the ensemble recovers 11 of them (+0.4 pp). Identifier blocking recovers 7 true pairs the HNSW ensemble missed on NCV (+0.3 pp) and adds nothing on DBLP-Scholar, which has no identifier fields. — [Pavani et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.26298v1)
- Same paper, precision after a tournament-winning matcher plus a global threshold. Cluster purity (%), Table V, columns Baseline / +Sparsity-aware thresholds / +Per-field hard vetoes: Restaurants 51.6 / 53.1 / 84.4; Cora 95.9 / 98.0 / 98.1; Geo Settlements 98.3 / 98.7 / 98.5; DBLP-Scholar 95.4 / 99.8 / 99.5; MusicBrainz 200K 77.7 / 86.1 / 89.7; NCV 97.4 / 98.9 / 98.6. Prose attributes +8.4 pp purity on MusicBrainz and +1.5 pp on NCV to the sparsity-specific threshold, and +3.6 pp on MusicBrainz and +32.8 pp on Restaurants to hard vetoes. The table increments for vetoes are +31.3 on Restaurants (53.1→84.4) and +3.6 on MusicBrainz (86.1→89.7); 84.4−51.6=32.8 is the baseline-to-final gap, so the prose and the table disagree by 1.5 pp on what the veto step itself did. On Geo, DBLP-Scholar, and NCV the veto column is 0.2–0.3 pp worse than sparsity alone. — [Pavani et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.26298v1)
- Same paper, clustering ablation, Table VI, pair F1 (center-based baseline / connected-components transitivity / verified merge): Restaurants 1.000 / 1.000 / 1.000; Cora 0.321 / 0.885 / 0.876; Geo Settlements 0.889 / 0.771 / 0.972; DBLP 0.424 / 0.371 / 0.477; MusicBrainz 200K 0.540 / 0.000 / 0.277; NCV 0.667 / 0.002 / 0.667. Prose: on Cora, validation precision 99.7%, transitivity is the large gain (0.321→0.885); on Geo, raw transitivity hurts (0.889→0.771) and verified merge wins (0.972); on MusicBrainz and NCV transitivity collapses F1 (0.540→0.000 and 0.667→0.002). Verified merge is best or tied-best on 4/6 datasets. These pair-F1 numbers are not the tournament matcher F1 in their Table III (MusicBrainz DeepMatcher 0.964, NCV LightGBM 0.993). Table VI’s own pairwise precision on the MusicBrainz baseline is 0.509; the authors say verified merge cannot save a matcher whose precision is that low. — [Pavani et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.26298v1)
- Same paper, matcher tournament under a ≤10K label cap, Table III pair F1: DeepMatcher and LightGBM split 3/6 wins; GAT wins none. Largest matcher-family gap on a single set is DBLP-Scholar, LightGBM 0.541 vs DeepMatcher 0.160. NCV is saturated: 0.992 / 0.993 / 0.989, margin under 0.001. This tournament is not conditioned on a published blocking-recall ≥98% cut. — [Pavani et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.26298v1)
- MERAI (Kannangara et al., arXiv:2508.03767, 5 Aug 2025; also IEEE BigData 2025) compares full pipelines to Dedupe and Splink on North Carolina voter snapshots, up to 15.7M records. It does not report blocking pair-completeness. At a Splink match-probability threshold of 0.99, the prose says Splink dedup precision is 60–76.5% with recall 85.7–98.4%, while MERAI dedup precision is 100% with recall 92.5–95%. On linkage, MERAI precision 92.6–96.7% and recall 98.2–100% give F1 above 96%; Dedupe recall is 99.4–100% but precision 71.6–73.7%; Splink precision 88.6–94.1% and recall 78.3–85.2%. The HTML table of exact per-snapshot F1 did not survive extraction, so those ranges are the prose, not a reconstructed table. MERAI’s matcher is Magellan-style string similarities (token and edit) plus a supervised classifier, not a neural embedder; clustering is weighted disjoint cliques rather than connected components. No component-level delta is given at fixed blocking recall. — [MERAI](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.03767)
- Sudowoodo (Wang et al., arXiv:2207.04122) reports blocking recall at or above 98% on some Magellan sets versus DL-Block: DBLP-ACM 99.6% recall for both, candidate count 13,100 vs 11,470; DBLP-Scholar 98.1% vs 98.4%, candidates 392,400 vs 257,052. The claimed gain is a smaller candidate set at similar recall (up to 84.8% smaller on Abt-Buy), not a matcher, threshold, clustering, or rule ablation after recall is frozen above 98%. — [Sudowoodo](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2207.04122)
- AnyMatch (arXiv:2409.04073) ablates the matcher and the synthetic training mix on already-built benchmark pair sets, not a measured blocker. Mean zero-shot F1 81.96. Replacing GPT-2 with T5 costs 2.38 mean F1; replacing it with BERT costs 9.04. Dropping all of their training-data generation steps costs 3.19 mean F1. MatchGPT with GPT-4 is 86.36 mean F1, 4.4 points above AnyMatch. Unicorn’s zero-shot mean on the same table is 77.54. None of these deltas are after a reported blocking recall above 98%. — [AnyMatch](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.04073v1)
- Ditto’s own ablations (Li et al., arXiv:2004.00584) are on benchmark pair sets. Domain-knowledge injection is +1.2% average F1 on ER-Magellan; data augmentation is +2.53% average on WDC; summarization is reported as +52% on one text-heavy set. Pretrained language models account for “over 50%” of Ditto’s gain versus prior matchers. These are matcher and augmentation deltas, not post-blocking-recall ablations. — [Ditto](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2004.00584)

### Inferences
- With pair completeness already 99.75%, the Walmart retrieval result is the right order of magnitude for a better embedder: their ensemble and identifier index, on top of a retriever already at 97.7%, bought +0.3 to +0.4 pp of recall and, on DBLP-Scholar, zero. That cannot fund a macro-F0.5 move of more than about a thousandth if the missing 0.25% of true pairs is the only recall left, unless those missed pairs are concentrated in one country and macro-averaging amplifies them. The papers do not measure that country-level amplification.
- The components that still move precision, which is what F0.5 weights twice as heavily as recall, are a threshold that depends on how many fields are populated, hard per-field vetoes, and a clustering rule that refuses transitive closure unless a direct cross-cluster check passes. A different neural matcher is not what their tables show at this operating point, except on sets where the matcher itself is still weak (their DBLP-Scholar tournament).
- Vetoes are not free precision. On three of six sets they cost 0.2–0.3 purity points. The large veto gain is on Restaurants, where identifier conflicts are common and baseline purity is 51.6.
- Do not read Table VI’s collapse (F1 to 0.000) as the delta on top of a 0.99-precision matcher. The authors tie that collapse to baseline pairwise precision around 0.5. The directional warning still applies to macro F0.5: one accepted false link can merge clusters, and F0.5 will punish that harder than F1.

### Gaps
- No Splink, Dedupe, or MERAI paper states blocking recall and then isolates matcher versus threshold versus clustering versus rules. MERAI’s exact per-dataset F1 table was garbled in the HTML conversion; only the authors’ prose ranges are safe to cite.
- Sudowoodo, Unicorn, MatchGPT, and AnyMatch evaluate matching on pre-materialized candidate sets. Those sets are not documented in the sources above as having pair completeness above 98% of all true pairs in the raw tables.
- The bipartite-matching literature fixes the scored graph and varies only the clustering algorithm, but this pass did not extract a numeric F1 delta from that line of work.
- No cited paper uses macro F0.5.

## Did refitting on a shifted validation set fail, with a simpler source-trained model winning?

### Takeaway
No entity-resolution paper found here refits on a validation set that was deliberately enriched with decoys and ownerless records and then loses to the untouched source model on the true shifted test. The closest measured patterns are: (1) on a real attribute-agreement shift, the smaller model beats the larger ones that fit the validation cue, and refitting only helps after the training distribution is corrected to the true shift; (2) fine-tuning on the source domain loses to zero-shot on cross-domain transfer; (3) in-distribution and out-of-distribution accuracy are sometimes inversely correlated, so a higher dev score selects the worse shifted model. A large body of work reports the opposite correlation.

### Cited Findings
- Zhang, Li, Calixto, Groth, and Schelter, “Beyond Scale and Generation: Understanding Language Model-based Entity Matching,” arXiv:2607.24688v1, 27 Jul 2026, https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.24688. On Semi-Rel (movie records), negative pairs with identical titles are 0.5% of training negatives and 0.4% of validation negatives, but 28.7% of test negatives (more than 70×). Table 6, original split, cross-encoder F1: 0.6B 97.6±0.2 (precision 96.9), 4B 89.5±4.7 (precision 81.7), 8B 85.0±11.4 (precision 76.4). Recall stays near 99. The smaller model wins because larger models lean harder on the title-agreement shortcut that is valid on the validation split and false on the test split. Generative matchers drop less (94.0 → 90.0 from 0.6B to 8B) but still do not beat the 0.6B cross-encoder. — [Zhang et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2607.24688)
- Same paper, the refit that matches the true shift does work. They rebuild Semi-Rel so the title-collision rate among negatives is 6.2% / 6.2% / 5.9% in train / valid / test. After fine-tuning on that rebalanced set, both architectures reach about 98 F1 at every size, with precision about 97–98. Table 6 rebalanced cross-encoder F1 is 98.2, 98.3, and 98.1 at 0.6B, 4B, and 8B. This is the opposite of a failed refit: the correction matched the actual shift mechanism (title collisions), not a generic increase in decoys. — [Zhang et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2607.24688)
- Same paper, a second shift (Semi-Heter): 82% of positive test pairs have attribute combinations absent from train and validation. Cross-encoder recall stays low (42.6 / 48.9 / 51.1 at 0.6B / 4B / 8B; F1 58.7 / 62.8 / 65.1) despite precision around 94. After they cut unseen attribute combinations in the positive test pairs from 82% to 17% and fine-tune on that rebalanced set, cross-encoder recall rises to about 96–97 and F1 to 97.1 / 97.3 / 96.1. Again the successful intervention edits the distribution to the true mismatch. It is not “add more hard negatives and hope.” — [Zhang et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2607.24688)
- Steiner, Peeters, and Bizer, “Fine-tuning Large Language Models for Entity Matching,” arXiv:2409.08185v2 (12 Sep 2024; revised 21 May 2025), ICDEW 2025, https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.08185. Fine-tuning improves in-domain transfer and hurts cross-domain transfer. Cross-domain performance “falls short of the zero-shot baseline in the majority of cases.” Product-to-scholar: Llama 3.1 8B models average −11.05 F1 versus their zero-shot baseline; all Llama 8B runs and half of the GPT-4o-mini runs underperform zero-shot. Scholar-to-product: Llama 8B averages −1.11 F1 versus zero-shot, while GPT-4o-mini averages +1.60. Llama 70B also fails to beat zero-shot on the cross-domain scenarios they report. The simpler, unfitted zero-shot model is the one that wins the shifted domain for the open 8B model. The fine-tune is on source labels, not on a decoy-enriched copy of the target. — [Steiner et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.08185v2)
- Teney, Lin, Oh, and Abbasnejad, “ID and OOD Performance Are Sometimes Inversely Correlated on Real-world Datasets,” NeurIPS 2023, arXiv:2209.00613, https://arxiv.org/abs/2209.00613. Inverse ID/OOD correlation is measured on real data (Camelyon17 and four of five other sets they discuss), especially across epochs, seeds, and a diversity regularizer. They conclude that using ID performance for model selection misses the best OOD models, and that pushing ID further can give negative returns on OOD. This is not entity resolution and not a decoy refit. It is the measured version of “a higher dev score ranked worse on the shifted test.” — [Teney et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2209.00613)
- Miller et al., “Accuracy on the Line,” arXiv:2107.04649, report the contradiction: strong positive ID–OOD correlation on CIFAR-10.1 (R² 0.995, 1,060 models), ImageNet-V2 (R² 0.996, 219 models), FMoW-WILDS (R² 0.984), and a weaker relationship on Camelyon17. A higher in-distribution score usually ranked higher out of distribution in their sample. — [Miller et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2107.04649v2)
- Gulrajani and Lopez-Paz, DomainBed (ICLR 2021), and follow-ups such as Zhu et al., arXiv:2510.04441 (6 Oct 2025), find that plain empirical risk minimization on the pooled source is hard to beat with domain-generalization training when the shift is covariate shift. Zhu et al. say that result is mainly for covariate shift, and that a domain-informed variant of ERM wins under posterior drift. Vision and language benchmarks, not entity resolution, and not a failed refit on extra decoys. — [DomainBed line](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.04441)
- An EDBT 2025 cross-dataset entity-matching study (DOI 10.48786/edbt.2025.75) says, describing prior LLM prompting work, that randomly selected demonstrations drawn from the target dataset can hurt versus prompting with no demonstrations. This pass did not open the underlying demonstration table, so the delta is not quoted here. The study’s own summary is that fine-tuned small models can match prompted large models and that data-centric choices beat model-centric ones. — [EDBT 2025 cross-dataset EM](http://dx.doi.org/10.48786/edbt.2025.75)

### Inferences
- The team’s backward dev-versus-leaderboard ranking has a documented cousin in Teney et al., and a documented opposite in Miller et al. It is not safe to treat “dev went up” as evidence the public score will go down, or the reverse. Two observed inversions are enough to stop trusting that particular dev set as a ranker, which is what Teney warns, without proving a general inverse law.
- Zhang et al. is the useful mechanism result. A validation set whose negative pairs almost never share a title (0.4%) will reward a model that treats title agreement as a match. The true test, where 28.7% of negatives share a title, then punishes that model, and larger models are punished more. Adding generic decoys would not have reproduced this unless the decoys carried the same spurious cue at the test rate. When the authors did put that cue into train and validation at the test rate, the refit worked and F1 returned to ~98. A failed refit on “more decoys and more ownerless records” is what you get when the proxy shift is the wrong shift, not evidence that any adaptation must fail.
- Steiner et al. is the clean “simpler model won” measurement inside entity matching: zero-shot beat source-domain fine-tuning on the cross-domain test, by about 11 F1 points for Llama 3.1 8B in the product-to-scholar direction. That is a source-trained (here, source-fine-tuned) model losing to a model that was not fitted to the source labels. It is not a refit on ownerless target records.
- No source supports retuning on the unlabelled test country. The successful Zhang refits used a redesigned labelled split whose bias was known exactly.

### Gaps
- No paper was found that adds ownerless records, or a higher decoy rate, to a validation set, refits, and reports a loss against the source-trained model on a held-out shifted test. That specific protocol remains the team’s unpublished result.
- The EDBT 2025 “target demonstrations can hurt” claim was only seen as a citation inside that paper’s PDF extract, without the original per-dataset deltas.
- Teney and Miller disagree, and both are outside entity resolution. Neither uses macro F0.5.

## Character-level or byte-level matchers versus word-level transformers on names with unseen vocabulary

### Takeaway
No benchmark found here pits a character-level or byte-level matcher against a word-level transformer on organization names, French descriptors, or legal-form tokens, with the winner under an Apache or MIT license and at most 8B parameters. On entity-matching benchmarks, character n-gram embeddings beat other static word embeddings on out-of-vocabulary tokens and still lose to contextual wordpiece or sentence transformers. On a multilingual NLI benchmark that does include French, a 300M byte-level model beat a 300M subword model. On Magellan’s dirty splits, a wordpiece transformer beat the earlier character-aware neural matcher by a wide margin.

### Cited Findings
- Zeakis, Papadakis, Skoutas, and Koubarakis, “Pre-trained Embeddings for Entity Resolution: An Experimental Analysis,” PVLDB 2023, arXiv:2304.12329, extended as VLDB Journal 34:5 (2025), https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.12329 and https://doi.org/10.1007/s00778-024-00879-4. Twelve models, 17 ER datasets. The VLDB Journal text states that fastText, whose vectors include character n-grams, consistently beats GloVe on these datasets because that character-level component handles domain out-of-vocabulary tokens, and that static embeddings (fastText, GloVe, word2vec) still typically underperform contextual BERT and Sentence-BERT models. In the unsupervised-matching comparison, S-GTR-T5 is the top model on seven of ten reported datasets. fastText is a static embedding model, not an ≤8B transformer matcher. License of the fastText code release is MIT; this pass did not re-download the LICENSE file. — [Zeakis et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.12329)
- Li et al., Ditto, arXiv:2004.00584, Table 5, dirty ER-Magellan F1. DeepMatcher+ (the prior neural matcher, which uses fastText character n-grams in the DeepMatcher design) versus Ditto: DBLP-ACM dirty 98.1 vs 99.03; DBLP-Google dirty 93.8 vs 95.75; iTunes-Amazon dirty 79.4 vs 95.65; Walmart-Amazon dirty 53.8 vs 85.69. The authors say Ditto’s average drop from clean to dirty is 0.57 F1, versus 8.21 for DeepMatcher+. The wordpiece transformer won on the dirty name and product strings. Ditto’s models (BERT, DistilBERT, RoBERTa) are Apache-2.0 and far under 8B. This is attribute-value noise, not a French legal-form vocabulary split. — [Ditto](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2004.00584)
- Sun, Fernandes, Wang, and Neubig, “A Multi-dimensional Evaluation of Tokenizer-free Multilingual Pretrained Models,” arXiv:2210.07111, 13 Oct 2022, https://arxiv.org/abs/2210.07111. Zero-shot XNLI (English fine-tune, 15 languages), not name matching. ByT5-Small, byte-level, 300M parameters: French 75.0, average 67.2. mT5-Small, subword, 300M: French 62.4, average 63.0. mBERT, subword, 178M: French 72.8, average 65.4. CANINE-S, character-level, 127M: French 66.0, average 53.8. CANINE-C: French 66.4, average 55.0. Byte-level ByT5-Small beat the same-size subword model and mBERT on French and on the average. The character encoder CANINE lost to mBERT. The authors’ own conclusion is that subword models may still be the practical choice because of inference latency and memory, not because they won this accuracy table. ByT5 and CANINE are Google Research releases distributed under Apache-2.0; both are under 8B (ByT5-small 300M, ByT5-base about 582M, ByT5-large about 1.2B, ByT5-xl about 3.7B; ByT5-xxl is about 13B and would violate the cap). This pass did not re-download LICENSE files. — [Sun et al. 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2210.07111)
- Clark et al., CANINE, arXiv:2103.06874. On TyDi QA, CANINE-S is +2.8 F1 over mBERT and CANINE-C + n-grams is +4.9 F1 (SelectP), at 127–167M parameters. On NER, CANINE-C alone drops CoNLL F1 from 87.8 to 74.0; adding character n-grams returns CoNLL to 86.7 (−1.1) and raises MasakhaNER from 72.4 to 76.8 (+4.3). Character-level wins on the multilingual NER set and loses or ties on English CoNLL. Not a name-matching task. Apache-2.0, under 8B. — [CANINE](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2103.06874)
- Höpner et al. (and co-authors), “Transformer-based Entity Legal Form Classification,” arXiv:2310.12766, https://arxiv.org/html/2310.12766v1. Legal-form classification from the raw legal name plus jurisdiction, ISO 20275 ELF codes, 1.1M entities, 30 jurisdictions. This is classification of the legal form, not a match decision between two records. WordPiece BERT beats a bag-of-words traditional model by a small margin: Germany, bert-base-german-uncased F1 0.9616 versus best traditional (random forest) 0.9537; macro F1 0.6174 versus 0.5906. Netherlands, multilingual BERT F1 0.9847 versus 0.9812. Italy, Italian BERT F1 0.9010 versus 0.8990, but macro F1 is worse (0.3121 versus 0.3218). No character-level or byte-level matcher is in the comparison. — [ELF classification](https://arxiv.org/html/2310.12766v1)
- Peeters and Bizer, “Cross-Language Learning for Entity Matching,” arXiv:2110.03338, https://arxiv.org/pdf/2110.03338v1. Product matching in German with and without English training pairs. The paper’s claim, which is the part that survived a messy table extract: with scarce target-language labels (they discuss 450 and 1,800 German pairs), adding English pairs raises F1 above 0.9 and beats a matcher trained only on the target language. One reported operating point is 1,800 German + 7,200 English. The models are wordpiece or sentencepiece multilingual transformers (mBERT, XLM-R), not byte-level. This result uses labelled target-language data plus extra English labels. It is recorded only as a contrary measurement. It is outside the team’s constraints and is not a recommendation. — [Peeters and Bizer 2021](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2110.03338v1)

### Inferences
- For entity matching as usually benchmarked, the published winner is the contextual wordpiece or sentence transformer, not the character n-gram embedding. The character n-gram advantage that does exist is against other static word vectors on out-of-vocabulary domain terms (Zeakis), and it is not large enough in those studies to pass BERT.
- The dirty Magellan result points the same way: Ditto’s wordpiece model lost less than 1 F1 point to attribute noise and beat DeepMatcher+ by 16 points on dirty iTunes-Amazon and 32 points on dirty Walmart-Amazon. If the unseen tokens are moved or misspelled attribute values of the Magellan-dirty kind, the literature says a small wordpiece cross-encoder already won.
- The case for bytes is narrower than “names.” ByT5-Small beat mT5-Small by 12.6 points on French XNLI and by 4.2 points on the 15-language average, at the same 300M size, Apache-2.0, under 8B. That is a language-transfer result, not a demonstration that a byte-level matcher would raise macro F0.5 on French legal-form strings after a 99.75% blocker. Sun et al. also say the accuracy win can be the wrong deployment choice because byte models are slower and heavier.
- Nothing in this section justifies training on the unlabelled test country or pulling in an external gazetteer of French legal forms.

### Gaps
- No head-to-head on organization names, French descriptors (SARL, SCI, and similar), or legal-form aliases was found with both a byte or character matcher and a word-level transformer, a reported F1, a model size ≤8B, and an Apache or MIT license.
- License statements for fastText, ByT5, CANINE, and Qwen3 were not re-checked against the LICENSE file in this pass. The papers and project releases are the standard Apache-2.0 (Google) and MIT (fastText) releases; Qwen3’s Apache-2.0 status was not opened as a separate page here.
- XLM-R’s license is not uniform across redistribution channels and was not verified.

## Strongest 2025–2026 claim on dirty entity matching (Magellan dirty, WDC), and the Qwen3-4B = 8B at 84.1 note

### Takeaway
The second-hand note is right about one cell and wrong about its scope. In Zhang et al. (27 Jul 2026) the Qwen3 cross-encoder’s macro F1 over nine datasets is 83.0 at 0.6B, 84.1 at 4B, and 84.1 at 8B. The tie disappears if the two shift datasets are removed (84.4 / 86.4 / 86.7), and it is not a tie for generative matchers (84.4 / 86.8 / 87.7). The study has no unseen language. It does not evaluate the Magellan dirty splits. On the hard WDC Products corner-case split it does evaluate, Qwen3 models pass that paper’s own Ditto re-run by a large margin. Ditto’s 2020 published dirty-Magellan numbers have not been replaced by a 2025–2026 table that this pass could quote.

### Inferences
- Correct the second-hand note as follows. Qwen3-4B and Qwen3-8B cross-encoders do tie at macro F1 84.1 in Zhang et al. 2026. That average is nine English datasets, not a dirty-Magellan macro and not an unseen-language macro. The tie is produced in part by Semi-Heter and Semi-Rel, where both sizes are weak or high-variance and move in opposite directions. Off those two sets, 8B still leads 4B (86.7 versus 86.4). Generative Qwen3 does not tie: 8B leads 4B by 0.9 macro F1 (87.7 versus 86.8). On the hard WDC split (80% corner cases) the ranking is generative 8B 83.9, cross-encoder 8B 83.2, generative 4B 82.6, zero-shot GPT 82.2, cross-encoder 4B 81.7, all well above that paper’s Ditto re-run of 57.9±21.4.
- Calling this a new state of the art on “dirty entity matching” is only fair for the WDC Products corner-case definition of dirty (hard negatives with high string overlap). It is not fair for Magellan dirty. Nobody in the 2025–2026 sources quoted here published iTunes-Amazon dirty, Walmart-Amazon dirty, and the two DBLP dirty sets above Ditto’s 95.65 / 85.69 / 99.03 / 95.75, except AttendEM’s half-point-scale claim against Ditto’s already published ER-Magellan average, without a dirty-only table in hand.
- For a cascade whose recall is already 99.75%, Zhang et al. is a matcher-architecture paper on pre-blocked pairs. It does not say that swapping a 4B cross-encoder for an 8B one, or a bi-encoder for a cross-encoder, moves end-to-end macro F0.5 once the candidate set is frozen. Their own bi-encoder versus cross-encoder gap (macro 81.5 versus 84.1 at 8B) is real on those candidate sets and would be relevant only to pairs the blocker already returned.
- The study does not contain an unseen language. It cannot be cited as evidence about the unlabelled test country.

### Gaps
- ChatMatcher’s December 2026 dirty-ER numbers were behind a paywall in this pass. If that table exists, it could supersede or contradict the “strongest claim” above.
- AttendEM’s dirty-only F1s, as opposed to the mean over all of ER-Magellan, were not extracted.
- No 2025–2026 paper found here reports macro F0.5 on Magellan dirty or on WDC.
- Ditto’s original WDC Computers numbers and Zhang et al.’s WDC Products w20/w80 numbers are different benchmarks. A single “WDC SOTA” sentence that mixes them would be false.
