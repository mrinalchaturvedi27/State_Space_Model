# Selective trust of one over-rejecting classifier

Scope: whether a classifier that is reliable in-domain and over-rejects out of domain can be ignored on a slice detected without target labels, for entity resolution scored by macro F0.5. Date of search: 2026-09-26. Already measured and closed by the team, and not recommended again: constant-likelihood prior correction (not identified); masking the judge at inference (costs 0.004–0.005 macro F0.5, and 0.04 on one bad stacker); clipping every veto in a known country (costs 0.0005–0.0015); a LoRA trained only on the previous model's mistakes (AUC 0.21, inverted). No published number below is a macro-F0.5 gain on this team's matcher. Where a paper reports a gain on a different metric or task, that is stated, and the entity-resolution F0.5 transfer is marked unmeasured.

## Which 2019–2026 methods let you ignore an expert on a subgroup detected without target labels, and what fails if both the prior and the true-positive rate move?

### Takeaway
No 2019–2026 selective-classification, learning-to-defer, or mixture-of-experts result both (i) ignores an expert on a subgroup you can see without target labels and (ii) reports a measured gain once the class prior and the expert's true-positive rate move together. The unlabeled signals that look usable — a country absent from training, or a veto rate far above the source rate — do not identify which of those two parameters moved. The theorems that would justify a correction all assume the expert's class-conditional behavior is fixed, which is exactly the assumption that fails in that case.

### Cited Findings
- Label-shift correction is consistent only if the class-conditional is invariant. Black-box shift estimation (BBSE) estimates the target label marginal from a black-box predictor's average outputs on unlabeled test data and the predictor's source confusion matrix, and is consistent when \(p(y)\) changes but \(p(x \mid y)\) does not, provided the confusion matrix is invertible. It does not require the predictor to be calibrated or accurate. The same confusion matrix is the expert's true-positive and false-positive rates. If those rates also change, the source matrix is the wrong linear map and the prior estimate is not consistent. — [Lipton, Wang, and Smola, ICML 2018](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v80/lipton18a.html); [arXiv:1802.03916](https://arxiv.org/abs/1802.03916)
- One observed rate cannot separate the two parameters. For a single binary agreement or veto, the mixture is \(P(\text{agree}) = m\pi + u(1-\pi)\), with \(m\) the match (true-positive) agreement probability, \(u\) the non-match agreement probability, and \(\pi\) the match prior. Under conditional independence, three binary comparison fields give seven equations and seven unknowns, so \(m\), \(u\), and \(P(M)\) are estimable together; that is the classical Fellegi–Sunter / EM setup. A single veto-rate spike is one equation. — [Winkler, Census RRS2006-02](https://www.census.gov/content/dam/Census/library/working-papers/2006/adrm/rrs2006-02.pdf), summarizing Fellegi–Sunter and the EM implementation in Winkler (1988)
- The match-conditional agreement rate is not stable across regions that look like a "new country." Winkler reports that optimal \(m\)- and \(u\)-probabilities "can vary substantially from one region of the U.S. to another," and that \(P(\text{agreement on first name} \mid M)\) "can differ significantly from an urban region to an adjacent suburban region," largely because typographical error rates differ across files. EM on the pair set is also unreliable as a latent-class method unless the matched-pair proportion in that set is above about 5%. — [Winkler, Census RRS2006-02](https://www.census.gov/content/dam/Census/library/working-papers/2006/adrm/rrs2006-02.pdf), citing Winkler (1989a)
- Unlabeled domain divergence does not by itself say the source expert is wrong. For a hypothesis class of VC-dimension \(d\), with unlabeled samples of size \(m'\) from source and target, with probability at least \(1-\delta\), for every hypothesis \(h\),
  \(R_T(h) \le R_S(h) + \tfrac{1}{2}\hat{d}_{\mathcal{H}\Delta\mathcal{H}}(S_u, T_u) + 4\sqrt{(2\,d\log(2m') + \log(2/\delta))/m'} + \lambda\),
  where \(\hat{d}_{\mathcal{H}\Delta\mathcal{H}}\) is estimated from unlabeled data only, and \(\lambda = \min_h [R_S(h) + R_T(h)]\) is the joint error of the best hypothesis on both domains. The paper's premise is that a source-trained classifier is controlled on the target only when some hypothesis does well on both domains. \(\lambda\) needs target labels. A change in the labeling function — the expert's true-positive rate moving, not only the prior — is exactly what makes \(\lambda\) large, and it is not estimable from the unlabeled divergence. — [Ben-David, Blitzer, Crammer, Kulesza, Pereira, and Vaughan, Machine Learning 2010](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10994-009-5152-4); bound restated as Theorem 10 in [Redko et al., arXiv:2004.11829](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2004.11829)
- Novelty rejection is the formalism that matches "country absent from training," and it does not need target labels on the new slice. The 2024 reject-option survey separates ambiguity rejection (the posterior is flat near a decision boundary) from novelty rejection ( \(x\) falls where there was little or no training data, so the learned map from \(x\) to \(y\) is unsupported). Detecting that a country code is outside the training support is a novelty check. The survey does not prove that the expert's error increased, and it reports no entity-resolution F-score. — [Hendrickx, Perini, Van der Plas, Meert, and Davis, Machine Learning 113:3073–3110, 2024](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10994-024-06534-x); preprint [arXiv:2107.11277](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2107.11277)
- Post-hoc selective classification under shift abstains the whole classifier; it does not turn one expert off and leave another on. Generalized selective classification rejects in-distribution mistakes, label-shifted points (labels outside the training label set, which the classifier always misses), and covariate-shifted points with one score. Margin scores on the raw logits are argued to be more reliable than softmax-response scores and are invariant to logit scale. The authors explicitly do not choose the deployment threshold: "we follow most existing work on SC and do not touch on issues such as how the calibration set should be constructed and how the threshold should be selected." Their calibration recipe (their Algorithm 1) still uses labeled calibration pairs. No macro-F0.5 or entity-matching number is given. — [Liang, Peng, and Sun, TMLR 2024, arXiv:2405.05160](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.05160)
- A threshold that helps average accuracy can hurt the worst slice, including making that slice worse as more points are rejected. Across CelebA, Waterbirds, CheXpert-device, CivilComments, and MultiNLI, softmax-response and MC-dropout selective classification raise average accuracy as coverage falls, but worst-group accuracy does not reliably rise. On CelebA it decreases. On Waterbirds, CheXpert-device, and CivilComments it "never [rises] by more than 10 points until the noisy low-coverage regime, and sometimes decreases." Proposition (translated log-concave margins): if the worst-group and other-group margin densities are log-concave and \(f_{\text{others}}(\tau) = f_{\text{wg}}(\tau - d)\), then for every threshold \(\tau \ge 0\) the worst-group selective accuracy is at most the group-agnostic reference. After group DRO equalizes full-coverage group accuracies, the same abstention then improves every group — but that training step uses group labels. — [Jones, Sagawa, Koh, Kumar, and Liang, ICLR 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2010.14134); numbers also in the authors' summary [SAIL blog, 13 Oct 2021](https://ai.stanford.edu/blog/sc-magnifies-disparities/)
- Learning to defer to an unseen expert needs a context set of that expert's decisions, not a label-free slice detector. L2D-Pop adapts the rejector from a small demonstration set of a never-before-seen expert (traffic signs, skin lesions). Expert-agnostic L2D reports up to a 28% relative improvement on unseen experts versus prior L2D methods, on HAM10000, Blood Cells, Retinal OCT, and Liver Tumours, by comparing classifier confidence with a Bayesian summary of expert accuracy. The 28% is relative system accuracy on medical imaging, not entity-resolution F0.5, and the summary is estimated from expert annotations. A training-free conformal deferral cuts expert labels by up to 91.3% (needs only 8.7% of the labels L2D uses to beat L2D accuracy in the low-data regime) on CIFAR-10H and HAM10000; it still uses expert labels, and the reported win is accuracy, not F0.5. — [Tailor et al., arXiv:2403.02683](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.02683); [Strong et al., arXiv:2502.10533](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2502.10533); [arXiv:2509.12573](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.12573v3)
- A selector trained to keep calibration on accepted points, including an out-of-domain test, reduced \(\ell_2\) calibration-error AUC by 18–25% versus heuristic baselines. That is a calibration gain on the accepted subset, not a license to drop an over-rejecting matcher, and not an F0.5 result. — [arXiv:2208.12084](https://arxiv.org/html/2208.12084v2)

### Inferences
- "Country not in the training support" is a legal unlabeled novelty flag in the Hendrickx sense. It does not identify a drop in true-positive rate. The same flag is also what you would see if only the covariate support changed and \(p(y \mid x)\) were unchanged, in which case Ben-David's \(\lambda\) can stay small and the in-domain expert should be kept. The team's already-measured country clip (a blanket veto override on a known country) lost 0.0005–0.0015 and is a different intervention from an unseen-country novelty reject; nothing above transfers that number to an unseen country.
- "Veto rate far above the source rate" is the left-hand side of \(m\pi + u(1-\pi)\). A higher veto rate is equally compatible with fewer true matches (prior) and with the expert agreeing less often on true matches (true-positive rate). BBSE and constant-likelihood prior correction are the attempts to untangle that equation, and both require the true-positive rate to stay put. That is the assumption that fails when both parameters move, which is why the closed prior correction was not identified. Do not reopen it.
- Learning-to-defer and mixture-of-experts gates in this period are trained on expert or task labels. None of the cited deferral gains (28% relative accuracy, 91.3% fewer labels, 18–25% calibration AUC) is a label-free ignore rule, and none is macro F0.5 on dirty entity matching. Treating them as such would invent a transfer.
- Selective classification's own negative theorem (Jones et al., translated log-concave margins) says a confidence threshold fit to improve average accuracy need not improve, and can worsen, the worse group. An over-rejecting out-of-domain slice is the setting where that failure shows up: the score used to abstain is not a score of expert reliability on that slice.

### Gaps
- No paper found that states a theorem of the form "if an observable subgroup has training density near zero, or if its veto rate exceeds the source rate by a stated amount, then discarding the expert's negatives raises F0.5 by a stated amount." The entity-resolution gain of novelty rejection is unmeasured.
- The suggested "Hu et al., when to trust the source model" paper was not found under that description. Nearby 2024–2026 hits are a different problem: few trusted target samples for source-free adaptation ([arXiv:2409.00741](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.00741)), trust-weighted feature discrepancy that still uses target pseudo-labels ([arXiv:2605.25119](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.25119v1)), and a world-model identifiability result that is not a classifier ([arXiv:2609.26293](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.26293)). None supplies an entity-resolution number.
- Mixture-of-experts gating that shuts off one expert using only an unlabeled subgroup detector, with a measured accuracy or F-score gain, was not found for 2019–2026. Standard gates are fit with the task loss.
- Liang et al. say margin scores beat softmax scores for generalized selective classification but the numeric AURC deltas were not extracted here. Even those deltas would be risk-coverage on vision and sentiment tasks, not macro F0.5.

## Is there a 2024–2026 stacking result where a gradient-boosted tree on a language-model score plus string features beats the language model alone on dirty entity matching?

### Takeaway
No such result was found. The 2024–2026 papers that put gradient boosting next to embeddings, or that compare language-model matchers with classical similarity features, are either a different task or show the language model ahead of the similarity-feature model. A gradient-boosted stack on a language-model score plus string features, beating that language model on dirty textual entity matching, remains unpublished in the sources searched, with no F1 or F0.5 delta to cite.

### Cited Findings
- The closest 2025 boosting paper is not dirty textual entity matching and does not stack a language-model match score with string features. Fedorchenko and Arefiev, CLEF 2025 animal re-identification, train LightGBM on pairwise features of pretrained visual embeddings (global descriptors plus local keypoints). On the private leaderboard their selected submission scores 0.618 and the best submission scores 0.629 (official CLEF metric, not F0.5). In their ablation, boosting at threshold 0.75 scores 0.629 private / 0.681 validation, versus an average meta-model at 0.608 private / 0.669 validation, both with global and local features and padding. Dropping local features collapses boosting to 0.322 private. They say this beats cosine thresholding and "simple cosine similarity between pretrained embeddings," but they do not publish the cosine baseline's score, and the embeddings are image descriptors, not a text language model. The task is wildlife individual ID. They finished 7th. — [Fedorchenko and Arefiev, CEUR-WS Vol-4038, paper 240](https://ceur-ws.org/Vol-4038/paper_240.pdf)
- A 2024–2025 embedding study of entity resolution does not add a gradient-boosted head on top of a language-model score. Zeakis, Papadakis, Skoutas, and Koubarakis compare 12 pretrained models on 17 benchmarks for blocking and for supervised and unsupervised matching, including a complementarity check of whether a syntactic model helps a language model find more blocking candidates. On supervised matching in the journal version, Ditto is the best reported average F1 at 0.93, then Unicorn and RoBERTa at 0.92; HierGAT, S-MiniLM, and Sudowoodo average 0.84, 0.83, and 0.80. Those are the matchers themselves, not a tree stacked on a language-model probability plus string features. — [Zeakis et al., The VLDB Journal 34:5, 2025](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00778-024-00879-4); conference version [arXiv:2304.12329](https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.12329)
- Where string-similarity features and a neural matcher were compared on the same dirty-style product benchmark, the hybrid similarity model did not beat the language model. On Amazon–Google, Magellan (similarity features, classical learner) F1 49.1, DeepMatcher hybrid F1 69.3, BERT F1 73.1, BERT-ER F1 75.3. The hybrid is a neural combination of attribute similarities and a learned representation, not a gradient-boosted tree on a frozen language-model score, and it loses to BERT by 3.8 F1 points on that dataset. — [Li, Miao, Wang, Sun, and Wang, BERT-ER](https://windofshadow.github.io/_pages/pdf/BERTer.pdf), citing Magellan (Konda et al. 2016) and Hybrid (Mudgal et al. 2018)
- The language-model matcher that replaced those classical systems reports large gains over them, not a gain from stacking a tree on the language model. Ditto, fine-tuning BERT, DistilBERT, or RoBERTa as a pair classifier, "outperforms previous state-of-the-art by up to 29% of F1" in the abstract; the body says the same comparison reaches "up to 31% in F1," and says Ditto "consistently performs better on dirty data." On a company match of 789K against 412K records it reports F1 96.5%. The previous state of the art it beats includes similarity-feature learners, not a stack that already contains Ditto's score. — [Li et al., arXiv:2004.00584](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2004.00584)
- 2024–2025 entity-matching papers that do beat strong neural matchers do it by changing the language-model procedure, not by stacking a tree. ComEM's selecting strategy improves average F1 by 16.02% over a matching prompt, and the full ComEM pipeline adds up to 4 F1 points over selecting alone (GPT-3.5 and GPT-4o-mini tables; supervised reference points include Ditto mean F1 80.69 and HierGAT 83.34). AnyMatch's data-centric fine-tuning is reported on par with prompted GPT-4 at far lower cost. Neither paper trains a gradient-boosted tree on a language-model score plus string features. — [Wang et al., arXiv:2405.16884](https://arxiv.org/html/2405.16884v3); [Peeters, Der, and Bizer, EDBT 2025](https://doi.org/10.48786/edbt.2025.75)
- Older gradient tree boosting for entity disambiguation is not this stack either. Structured gradient tree boosting reports in-KB accuracy 93.0 on AIDA-test versus 92.2 for Ganea and Hofmann (2017), and 95.9 with PPRforNED candidate selection versus a prior published 94.0. Features are mention–entity relatedness, not a modern language-model match probability plus string similarities on a dirty entity-matching benchmark. — [Yang, Zhang, and Han, arXiv:1802.10229](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1802.10229)

### Inferences
- The searched 2024–2026 literature does not contain the requested positive stacking result. The one gradient-boosting "entity matching" paper from 2025 is animal re-identification: LightGBM versus averaging the same visual features gains 0.021 private score (0.629 vs 0.608) at one threshold, which is not a win over a text language model and not macro F0.5.
- The older comparisons that do put string features next to neural matchers go the other way on the published tables: hybrid similarity features trail BERT (69.3 vs 73.1 F1 on Amazon–Google), and Ditto's reported gains are over similarity-feature systems, not over a pipeline that already has the language-model score as a feature. That is evidence against expecting a tree on top of a strong language-model score to be the missing precision lever, but it is not a 2024–2026 measurement of that exact stack. The delta for this team's stacker remains unmeasured in the literature.

### Gaps
- No paper was found, in 2024–2026 or earlier in this search, that reports F1 or F0.5 for XGBoost, LightGBM, or CatBoost trained on a frozen language-model match score concatenated with string similarities, against the language model alone, on a dirty entity-matching benchmark (Magellan, WDC, DeepMatcher dirty splits, or similar). Absence is over arXiv, VLDB/VLDBJ, EDBT 2025, CLEF 2025 working notes, and the embedding survey above, not over every paywalled workshop paper.
- Zeakis et al. say they test syntactic-plus-language-model complementarity for blocking. The blocking delta (how many extra true pairs the syntactic model adds) was not in the open snippets used here. That experiment is candidate generation, not a gradient-boosted matcher, so it would not answer the question even with the number filled in.
- A 2025 Chinese patent describes Word2Vec plus cosine, BM25, and Jaccard into an MLP classifier ([CN120951001B](https://eureka.patsnap.com/patent/CN120951001B)). It is not a gradient-boosted tree, not a language-model score, and it publishes no F1. It is not a stacking result.

## Abstention and F-beta: thresholding for F0.5, and is the empty match set the precision lever?

### Takeaway
The published optimal-threshold theorem is for F1, not F0.5, and it does not say "return the empty set." For a calibrated score the F1-maximizing rule is "predict positive when the score is at least half the F1 you will achieve," and an uninformative classifier maximizes expected F1 by predicting every example positive. That is the opposite of using the empty prediction as a precision lever. No paper found shows that returning an empty match set, scored as 1.0 when the entity truly has no match, is the lever that maximizes macro F0.5 on entity resolution. The nearest measured "predict nothing" results are entity-linking NIL detectors, on a different metric, and they need NIL labels or a labeled threshold, not a label-free rule.

### Cited Findings
- For any real-valued score, Lipton, Elkan, and Naryanaswamy derive the threshold that maximizes F1. An example with score \(s\) is called positive by an F1-maximizing rule if and only if the likelihood ratio \(b \cdot p(s \mid t=1) / ((1-b) \cdot p(s \mid t=0))\) is at least a constant \(J\) fixed by the operating point. If the score is a calibrated probability, that rule is \(s \ge F/2\), where \(F\) is the F1 the same rule achieves. The threshold is therefore tied to the F1 value; in practice they sweep thresholds on a labeled batch. — [Lipton, Elkan, and Naryanaswamy, ECML PKDD 2014](https://arxiv.org/abs/1402.1892); [Springer chapter](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-662-44851-9_15)
- The same paper's uninformative-classifier case points away from the empty set. If the score carries no information about the label, the decision that maximizes expected F1 is to classify every example positive. They warn this is undesirable when the positive base rate is low, and that macro-averaged F1 over rare labels makes the all-positive rule especially harmful, while micro-F1 still likes it. They do not analyze F0.5, and they do not define an empty-versus-empty score of 1. — [Lipton et al., arXiv:1402.1892](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1402.1892)
- Pairwise entity-matching F1, the metric Ditto and the embedding surveys use, does not award 1.0 for "no pair predicted, and there was no pair." Precision and recall are computed from matched pairs. A true non-match that is left unmatched is a true negative and does not enter the F1 numerator. Predicting no matches at all drives recall to 0 whenever any true match exists. Ditto and Zeakis et al. report that pair-F1, not a per-entity macro F0.5 with the empty-empty convention. — [Li et al., arXiv:2004.00584](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2004.00584); [Zeakis et al., VLDB Journal 2025](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00778-024-00879-4)
- Industry guidance says to tune the auto-merge threshold for F0.5 when false merges are expensive, and to send the rest to review. It states the definition \(F_{0.5}\) weights precision twice recall, and it does not show that the maximizing action is an empty match set, nor any before/after F0.5 from emitting empty sets. — [Tilores, 20 Feb 2025](https://tilores.io/content/precision-and-recall-in-entity-identity-resolution/)
- Entity linking is the literature where "predict no entity" is an explicit class. BLINKout matches a mention to a special NIL entity instead of thresholding the top candidate alone. The authors say current practice is "mostly limited to the simple threshold-based approach," and that a NIL representation "performed better than or on par with threshold-based approaches." They report about a 30–40% improvement in out-of-KB F1 (\(F1_o\)) versus a rule-based baseline on MM-2014AB and NILK-sample. The gain is out-of-KB F1 on clinical, biomedical, and Wikipedia entity linking, not macro F0.5 entity resolution, and the NIL class is trained, not chosen as an empty set with no labels. — [Dong, Chen, He, Liu, and Horrocks, CIKM 2023, arXiv:2302.07189](https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.07189)
- Scoring NIL changes the headline number sharply, which is evidence that "always link" inflates accuracy, not that the empty decision is free precision. On Zhu et al.'s tables, BLINK's bi-encoder overall accuracy on AIDA is 82.61 without NIL mentions and 65.01 overall once NIL mentions are included; NIL accuracy on that split is 69.59, and on their NEL set NIL accuracy is 88.59 with overall accuracy 77.74. CLINK's cross-encoder reaches NIL accuracy 89.19 and overall 87.71 on NEL. They also report that NIL accuracy collapses unless Non-Entity Phrase examples are in training, and that 25% of those examples restores a "decent" NIL accuracy. Both NIL types in the training data matter. — [Zhu, Yu, Jin, Hou, Li, and Sui, ACL Findings 2023, arXiv:2305.15725](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2305.15725)
- A 2026 historical-entity-linking paper states, without a full numeric table in the available text, that an adaptive NIL threshold helps only when two conditions hold at once: a substantial share of mentions are NIL, and the bi-encoder's confidence is reliable because entities are mostly popular. When NIL is rare, or entities are long-tail, sending everything through the LLM is better. Again this is entity linking, not entity-resolution macro F0.5. — [arXiv:2601.08500](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.08500v1)

### Inferences
- Sweeping a threshold on labeled validation to maximize F1 is a published procedure (Lipton et al.). The same sweep can be pointed at F0.5, because F0.5 is still a function of the precision-recall pair induced by a threshold, but that extension is not a theorem in the paper, and no entity-resolution paper in this search reports the F0.5-maximizing threshold or shows that the optimum is the empty prediction. Mark the F0.5 sweep on this matcher as unmeasured in the literature.
- Under the F1 theory, the empty prediction (call nothing a match) is not the optimum for an uninformative or poorly separated score; the optimum in that case is to call everything a match. F0.5 weights precision more, so the optimal threshold moves up relative to F1, but "moves up" is not "emit the empty set," and there is no cited proof that empty-empty = 1.0 is what buys the precision. If this team's scorer really assigns 1.0 to an entity whose gold match set and predicted match set are both empty, that convention is stricter than pairwise F1 and is not what Ditto, Zeakis, Lipton, or the NIL papers evaluate. Whether empty-set prediction is the precision lever for that convention is unmeasured.
- NIL prediction is the nearest empirical cousin, and its measured gains (the 30–40% out-of-KB F1, and the drop from 82.61 to 65.01 overall accuracy once NIL is scored) require either a trained NIL class or a threshold fit on labeled data. They do not justify a label-free empty-set rule on an out-of-domain country.

### Gaps
- No paper found that maximizes F0.5, as opposed to F1, by threshold search on an entity-resolution benchmark, with the empty match set included as a candidate decision and with the empty-empty case defined as 1.0.
- No measurement found of how much macro F0.5 moves when the system is forced to return empty on low-confidence entities. The NIL numbers above are a different task and a different F.
- The cost-sensitive reduction of general \(F_\beta\) (line search over false-positive/false-negative costs) was not re-read in this pass. Lipton's closed form is stated only for \(\beta = 1\). A claimed F0.5 threshold formula without that source would be invented, so it is omitted.

## Group-conditional thresholds fit on the worse of two source groups, then used on an unseen group

### Takeaway
No result was found that fits a threshold on the worse of two source groups and shows that this maximin threshold is the right threshold for an unseen group. The closest theorems say the opposite: a threshold that helps on average need not help the worse group, and when the worse group's confidence distribution is only a shift of the others', no threshold beats a group-agnostic reference on that group. Model selection that uses source-domain validation, which is the practical stand-in for "tune on the sources, test on an unseen domain," stays near chance on ColoredMNIST when the unseen domain flips the correlation.

### Cited Findings
- Jones et al. prove a case in which every threshold fails the worse group. If the margin distributions are log-concave and one is a translation of the other, worst-group selective accuracy is less than or equal to the group-agnostic reference at every \(\tau \ge 0\). A necessary condition for the selective rule to beat that reference requires the worse group's margin distribution to be a contraction of the others' (\(v < 1\)), which their Waterbirds margins violate because the worse group has higher variance. Empirically, worst-group accuracy rises by at most about 10 points until coverage is so low the curve is noisy, and on CelebA it falls as the model abstains more. The repair they demonstrate is group DRO at training time so that full-coverage accuracies already match; abstention then improves each group. That uses group labels on the groups being scored, not a threshold copied from two source groups onto a third, unseen group. — [Jones et al., ICLR 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2010.14134); [SAIL blog](https://ai.stanford.edu/blog/sc-magnifies-disparities/)
- Tuning on source domains and evaluating on a held-out domain does not recover the oracle operating point when the unseen domain disagrees with the sources. DomainBed's training-domain validation protocol pools validation splits from the training domains and picks the model that maximizes that pooled accuracy, then tests on a domain that was hidden. Under that protocol, ERM on ColoredMNIST is \(52.0 \pm 0.1\). The ICLR write-up states that no algorithm in the suite beats ERM by more than one point on average under matched conditions. ColoredMNIST is the case where the label–color correlation reverses in the unseen domain, so a threshold or model chosen on the source groups is choosing the spurious rule. — [Gulrajani and Lopez-Paz, ICLR 2021, arXiv:2007.01434](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2007.01434); [ICLR abstract](https://iclr.cc/virtual/2021/poster/2998)
- The same validation-versus-unseen gap appears for a plain score threshold in the 2025 boosting re-identification paper, on one dataset rather than two source groups. The threshold 0.6 that is best on their validation split scores 0.686 validation and 0.618 private; the threshold 0.75 scores 0.681 validation and 0.629 private. They attribute the swap to the private split not matching the validation distribution. This is not a maximin over groups, and the metric is the CLEF animal-ID score, but it is a measured case of the validation-optimal threshold not being optimal on the unseen split (0.618 vs 0.629 private). — [Fedorchenko and Arefiev, CEUR-WS Vol-4038](https://ceur-ws.org/Vol-4038/paper_240.pdf)
- Generalized selective classification still picks its cutoff on a labeled calibration set drawn for a target coverage or risk, and the authors leave "how the threshold should be selected" under shift as future work. They do not fit the cutoff on the worse source group. — [Liang, Peng, and Sun, arXiv:2405.05160](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.05160)
- A September 2026 statistics paper adapts a threshold to an observed context variable \(Z\) so that conditional label distributions stay representative while a false-alarm rate is held fixed, with nonasymptotic error bounds. On the FICO credit example they say the adapted rule "can compete with" transformers. They do not report a numeric delta in the abstract, and the context must be observed at training time so the conditional threshold can be estimated. That is not "fit one number on the worse of two source groups, apply it to a group that was never seen." — [Steland, arXiv:2609.26652](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.26652)

### Inferences
- Copying the threshold that is best on the worse source group onto an unseen group is not a published estimator with a guarantee. Jones et al. give a sufficient condition under which no threshold, including one tuned to the worse group, raises that group's accuracy above the group-agnostic baseline: the worse group's confidence distribution is a translate of the others'. An unseen country whose score distribution has shifted but has the same shape is that case. The assumption that fails, again, is that the score-to-label map (true-positive rate at a given score) is the same as on the source groups. A prior shift alone would move mass between groups; a true-positive-rate shift moves the margin distribution. If both move, a threshold estimated on the worse source group is not the target group's F0.5 threshold, and unlabeled data on the unseen group does not identify the new threshold.
- DomainBed's ColoredMNIST number (52.0% under training-domain validation) is the empirical version of that failure for model selection rather than for a single threshold: when the unseen domain reverses the source correlation, source-group validation does not find a rule that works there. It is not a macro-F0.5 measurement.
- The CLEF threshold swap (validation prefers 0.6, private prefers 0.75, private scores 0.618 vs 0.629) is a small, real illustration that the threshold which maximizes a validation score is not the threshold which maximizes the unseen split. It is not evidence that the worse of two source groups supplies the missing threshold.

### Gaps
- No paper found that (i) estimates a threshold on each of two labeled source groups, (ii) keeps the threshold from the worse group, and (iii) reports accuracy, F1, or F0.5 on a third group with no labeled examples. The gain or loss of that maximin rule on entity resolution is unmeasured.
- DomainBed discusses leave-one-domain-out and an oracle that validates on the test domain. A numeric oracle-minus-training-domain gap of "about 2 points" appears only in a secondary overview of the paper, not in the primary passages used here, so it is not cited as a result. The primary figures used above are ERM at \(52.0 \pm 0.1\) on ColoredMNIST and the one-point algorithm gap.
- Nothing in this section reinstates the closed country clip. That experiment overrides every veto inside a known country and loses 0.0005–0.0015. A maximin threshold for an unseen country is a different rule, and it has no published F0.5.
