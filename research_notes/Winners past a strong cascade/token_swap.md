# Same-address, one-token name difference: descriptor swap vs different business

Scope: published record-linkage and entity-matching evidence on pairs that share a house number and street while the names differ by exactly one token (a descriptor such as Collège vs Pharmacie, a legal form, or another label). No external business registry or geocoder is usable by this team; gazetteer-backed systems are cited only to mark them out of scope. Date of search: 2026-09-26. Ideas with no measured precision or recall are marked unmeasured.

The literature’s “token swap” is usually forename/surname order inversion, not a substituted descriptor. Those are different errors and are kept separate below.

## What do record-linkage and address papers say about “same establishment, different descriptor” versus “different businesses in one building”? Is there a measured precision for a same-address, different-token rule?

### Takeaway
Papers treat the two situations as different problems, and they separate them by which token changed, not by the mere fact that one token changed. A substituted legal form or background descriptor is usually the same entity; a substituted core name at one street is often a different establishment. No 2020–2026 paper found here publishes precision for the exact rule “same house number and street, names differ by exactly one token.”

### Cited Findings
- Dalvi, Olteanu, Raghavan, and Bohannon (WWW 2014) define a place name as one core token plus background tokens. In “Fresca’s Peruvian Restaurant,” Fresca’s is the core and “Peruvian Restaurant” is the description. “Guggenheim” is the core of “Guggenheim Art Museum, Manhattan,” but the background of “Guggenheim Starbucks.” They report a recall of 90% at a precision of 90% “based solely on names,” better than TF-IDF weighting. The same paper’s examples are the two errors in this task: high string similarity can still be two different stores in one mall (“Newpark Mall Sears” vs “Newpark Mall Sears Outlet”), and “Central Park” vs “Central Park Cafe” are different places in New York and the same place almost everywhere else. Word weights are contextual, not a single global weight. — [Dalvi et al. 2014 PDF](https://archives.iw3c2.org/www2014/proceedings/proceedings/p409.pdf)
- The US Census Longitudinal Business Database links single-unit establishments that lost their numeric id by name and address, after identifier passes, expressly to avoid spurious births and deaths. Before fuzzy matching it deletes common words such as “the,” “of,” “company,” and “LLC.” After the probabilistic passes it runs four common-word passes: block on same state, standardized street at SAS DQMATCH sensitivity 70, and 5-digit ZIP; then require at least two common words (name tokens at sensitivity 95) that are unique inside that block. A pair is not kept merely because it shares the street. The write-up does not give precision or recall for those four passes. — [Chow et al., NBER w28839 / CES LBD, 2021](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w28839/w28839.pdf)
- Person-to-establishment linkage into the same Business Register is much less precise than a naive reading of “name plus address” suggests. On CenHRS, the tuned establishment model at probability 0.095 links 66.1% of respondents at realized precision 0.569 and realized recall 0.786 (limiting precision 0.724). Linking everyone has realized precision 0.034. Features include a cubic spline of Jaro–Winkler on the employer name and on the street, not a one-token rule. This is a person-to-workplace task, not business-to-business deduplication. — [Abowd et al., Boston Fed WP 22-11, Table 4](https://www.bostonfed.org/-/media/Documents/Workingpapers/PDF/2022/wp2211.pdf)
- For people, the same street is not an entity key. Enamorado, Fifield, and Imai report that street number + ZIP + gender is unique for only about 20% of Texas voters, while the address/DOB/gender/name combinations they actually link on are unique for over 99%. Christen’s population-reconstruction setting treats co-residents as different people and uses the household as a group to link, not as proof of identity. — [ADGN, Statistics and Public Policy 2017](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/2330443X.2017.1389620); [Christen 2016, arXiv 1612.04286](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1612.04286)
- Splink’s shipped name comparison is not a descriptor-substitution rule. `NameComparison` levels are null, exact, then Jaro–Winkler at 0.92, 0.88, and 0.70, optionally plus a Double Metaphone array intersection. `ForenameSurnameComparison` adds exact forename, exact surname, and a reversed-columns level (`first_name_l = surname_r` and `first_name_r = surname_l`). That reversed level is the literature’s “token swap”: order inversion of two person-name fields. Term-frequency adjustments down-weight an agreement on a common full string, using frequencies in the file being linked, not an external list. There is no built-in level for “all tokens agree except one.” — [Splink NameComparison](https://moj-analytical-services.github.io/splink/api_docs/comparison_library.html); [out-of-the-box comparisons](https://moj-analytical-services.github.io/splink/topic_guides/comparisons/out_of_the_box_comparisons.html); [term frequency](https://moj-analytical-services.github.io/splink/topic_guides/comparisons/term-frequency.html); [feature engineering, reversed columns](https://moj-analytical-services.github.io/splink/topic_guides/data_preparation/feature_engineering.html)
- Legal-form tokens are treated as non-identifying noise in company-name work, which is the “same establishment, different descriptor” side. Kruse, Awick, Marx Gómez, and Loos (BIS 2021) extract the legal form from the company name so that the cleaned name can be matched separately. Their best legal-form classifier, an SVC on legal-form tokens, has precision 0.9969, recall 0.9971, weighted F1 0.9970; a Bi-LSTM is described at about 99.2% F1. Those figures are classification of the legal-form tag, not precision of a subsequent match. A later summary says their tag recognition reached 98% F1; that is a different rounding or sub-task, not a linkage delta. — [Kruse et al. 2021, via ResearchGate text](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/352946513_Developing_a_Legal_Form_Classification_and_Extraction_Approach_for_Company_Entity_Matching_Benchmark_of_Rule-Based_and_Machine_Learning_Approaches); [Hoepner et al., arXiv 2310.12766](https://arxiv.org/html/2310.12766v1)
- Firm-level harmonization deliberately collapses legal-form and geographic descriptors (“Pfizer Limited,” “Pfizer, Inc.,” “Pfizer Italia S.r.l.” → Pfizer). Terrorizer reports about a 42% reduction in assignee-name strings on USPTO data. That decision is correct for a corporate family and wrong for an establishment: the Census linker above refuses to name-and-address-match multi-unit firms and requires the shared words to be unique on the street. — [Terrorizer, arXiv 2403.12083](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.12083v1); [Chow et al. 2021](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w28839/w28839.pdf)
- Gallego and coauthors’ company linker (arXiv 1907.08667) strips legal-entity tokens when it builds a family short name and reports recall 91% against 73% for their baseline. Again a firm-level recall, not a same-address one-token precision. — [arXiv 1907.08667](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1907.08667)
- Libpostal’s near-duplicate hasher drops ignorable street tokens (“Ave,” “Road”) using its own dictionaries and cites Dalvi et al. for geo-specific scores. That is an external lexicon plus coordinates. Out of scope for this team; included only as the postal engineering analogue. — [libpostal v1.1 notes](https://github.com/openvenues/libpostal/releases/tag/v1.1)

### Inferences
- A one-token difference is not one class. If the substituted token is background (legal form, “Restaurant,” “Cafe”) and the shared token is a rare core that is unique on that street, Dalvi and the Census common-word pass both point to the same establishment. If the substituted token is the core (“Starbucks” vs “Subway”) or the only shared tokens are common words that also occur elsewhere in the street/ZIP block, both point to different businesses in one building.
- The Census operational rule is stricter than “exactly one token differs”: it demands two surviving common words, unique inside the street-70 + ZIP5 block, and it applies only to single-unit establishments. “Collège Saint-Joseph” vs “Pharmacie Saint-Joseph” shares only one content word after “Saint-Joseph” is one token, so that pair would not fire the published Census pass. “Collège Saint Joseph” vs “Pharmacie Saint Joseph” shares two words and might, if those two words are unique on the block. Unmeasured on this team’s data.
- Splink will not, out of the box, score a descriptor substitution as its own agreement level. Jaro–Winkler also down-weights a change at the start of the string, so “Pharmacie X” vs “Collège X” can look dissimilar even when the distinctive token matches. A custom comparison level would be new work, not a documented Splink result.

### Gaps
- No retrieved paper states a precision for “same house number and street, names differ by exactly one token,” including none in 2020–2026. The Census common-word pass is the closest operational rule and publishes no precision. Dalvi’s 90/90 figure is a name matcher with contextual weights, not that rule, and the paper does not isolate a no-geography ablation in the pages retrieved.
- No Christen passage retrieved here measures business descriptor swaps. The opened Christen paper is about people and households.
- No Royal Mail, USPS, or other postal specification retrieved here gives a precision for this pattern. Libpostal is dictionary-based and out of scope.
- Kruse et al. do not report linkage precision before versus after legal-form extraction. The 99.7% F1 must not be read as match precision.

## Which name-only signals separate a descriptor swap from a different business, with no external gazetteer?

### Takeaway
The measured name-only signal is the weight of the shared tokens versus the weight of the one substituted token, estimated from the names in hand. IDF or a core/background model does that. Character edit distance and character bigrams help when the substituted token is a typo and hurt when it is a different word. A hand-built legal-form list is a lexicon; learning token frequency from the linkage file is not.

### Cited Findings
- Cohen, Ravikumar, and Fienberg (IJCAI workshop 2003) compare edit distance, token distances, and hybrids on name matching. The best average method is SoftTF-IDF: TF-IDF over tokens, with two tokens allowed to count as a soft match when Jaro–Winkler is high. Jimenez, Gonzalez, and Gelbukh (2010) rerun those sets. Average maximum F1 across the twelve sets: character-bigram cosine 0.717, SoftTF-IDF 0.808, their best soft-cardinality variant (SC3, with IDF) 0.827. Average interpolated average precision: bigrams 0.678, SoftTF-IDF 0.760, SC3 0.776. On the Business names set, interpolated average precision is 0.529 for bigrams, 0.533 for plain cosine, 0.704 for SoftTF-IDF, and 0.776 for SC3. On Census personal names the order flips: bigrams 0.806 versus SoftTF-IDF 0.726. Restaurants, which mix extra words and typos, are already high for both (bigrams 0.860, SoftTF-IDF 0.904). — [Jimenez et al. 2010 PDF](http://www.gelbukh.com/CV/Publications/2010/Text%20Comparison%20Using%20Soft%20Cardinality.pdf); [Cohen et al. 2003, cited there](https://www.cs.cmu.edu/~wcohen/postscript/ijcai-ws-2003.pdf)
- Cohen’s SecondString implementation treats tokens as a soft match only if Jaro–Winkler is at least 0.9 (the class comment says 0.9 or 0.95 “seems to be about right” on the WHIRL sets). A substituted token below that threshold contributes nothing; only the other tokens’ IDF-weighted overlap remains. “Collège” versus “Pharmacie” fails that inner threshold. Whether the pair still scores as a match then depends on the IDF of the surviving tokens. That threshold is an author default, not a reported precision. — [SoftTFIDF.java](https://sources.debian.org/src/libsecondstring-java/0.1~dfsg-2/com/wcohen/ss/SoftTFIDF.java)
- Dalvi et al. show why raw TF-IDF is the wrong weight for descriptors. “Peruvian” can be almost as rare as “Fresca” and so TF-IDF over-weights a background word. Their core/background model is learned from the places collection itself, without a hand lexicon. Combined with contextual weights it is the 90% precision at 90% recall system above. They also note that a single fixed weight per word cannot express “Cafe” being discriminative next to “Central Park” in New York and ignorable elsewhere. — [Dalvi et al. 2014](https://archives.iw3c2.org/www2014/proceedings/proceedings/p409.pdf)
- Splink term-frequency adjustment is the Fellegi–Sunter version of the same idea and is computed from the columns in the linkage, not from a gazetteer. It adjusts the agreement weight. It does not by itself label the disagreed token as a legal form. A partial-agreement level has to be declared explicitly (exact, Jaro–Winkler band, or a custom SQL predicate). — [Splink term frequency](https://moj-analytical-services.github.io/splink/topic_guides/comparisons/term-frequency.html)
- A 2026 vendor note argues for the same mechanism without a legal-form list: weight(token) = log(N / count(token)) on the customer’s own names. It says legal-form stripping does nothing for “Group,” “Holdings,” “Solutions,” which it claims occur in 5–25% of records and still carry meaning. No precision table is published on that page. Treat 5–25% and the precision claim as an unmeasured vendor statement. — [Tilores, 18 May 2026](https://tilores.io/content/company-name-normalization-isnt-enough-for-fuzzy-matching/)
- Hand legal-form lists are lexicons, even when small. Kruse et al. classify tokens against a maintained indicator list (`ag`, `aktiengesellschaft`, `gmbh`, …) and then an SVC. Hoepner et al. classify ISO 20275 entity-legal-form codes on 1.1 million LEI names from 30 jurisdictions; the ground truth is the GLEIF code list. ISO 20275, as described by a 2026 industry page, is a catalogue of more than 3,600 forms. None of these is “learned only from training match pairs,” and the code list is an external lexicon this team cannot use. — [Kruse dissertation excerpt citing the 2021 list](https://uol.de/f/2/dept/informatik/download/Promotionen/Diss_Kruse.PDF); [Hoepner et al., arXiv 2310.12766](https://arxiv.org/html/2310.12766v1); [IVRistech on ISO 20275, 12 Aug 2026](https://ivristech.com/fuzzy-string-matching-company-names/)
- Yang, Hoang, Mikolov, and Han (arXiv 1910.04861) train embeddings on place names and warn that semantic neighbors are the wrong inductive bias for address tokens: “street,” “avenue,” and “boulevard” should not be pulled together. They use embeddings to absorb typos, acronyms, and synonyms inside names, and they use the address to separate branches of one chain. No precision is given for a one-token substitution rule. — [arXiv 1910.04861](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1910.04861)

### Inferences
- For a single substituted token, three name-only scores answer different questions, and the papers measure them on different tasks. Character n-gram cosine and Levenshtein ask “is the differing token a misspelling?” SoftTF-IDF with a 0.9 inner Jaro–Winkler asks the same question per token, then weights the rest by IDF. Token-set overlap with IDF, or Dalvi’s core indicator, asks “is the surviving token the rare identifier, and is the dropped token background?” Only the third separates Collège/Pharmacie (different words, shared rare name) from Starbucks/Subway (different cores).
- A legal-form list learned only from labeled match pairs is an unmeasured design. What is measured is unsupervised IDF or core/background estimation on the name collection itself, and supervised legal-form tagging against a hand list or ISO codes. The hand list is disallowed; the frequency estimate is not a gazetteer.
- Edit distance on the one differing token is the inner comparator of SoftTF-IDF, not a published standalone rule with its own precision. Using it as a hard veto (large edit ⇒ different business) would, on the Cohen business-name evidence, reject true descriptor swaps that bigram cosine already ranks poorly (Business IAP 0.529).

### Gaps
- No paper retrieved reports precision and recall for “edit distance of the single substituted token” as its own decision rule, nor for token-set ratio specifically on same-address pairs.
- No paper retrieved induces a legal-form or descriptor lexicon only from labeled match pairs and then publishes match precision. Kruse’s 99.7% is tag classification against a hand list.
- Dalvi’s 90/90 result uses contextual weights learned with the places’ locations. A name-only, no-coordinate precision for the core model alone was not in the retrieved pages.
- Tilores’ IDF claim has no published precision table.

## Did any system win by overruling a language model on high-similarity pairs, or by trusting the model only below a similarity threshold?

### Takeaway
No retrieved system wins by overturning a language model’s “non-match” when a string score is high. The measured uses of string similarity are blocking, example selection, and features inside a classical learner. Where a paper does change the model’s behavior on close pairs, it puts the rule in the prompt or changes the training mix. A 2026 non-peer-reviewed demo is a warning that a very high string score can be the wrong entity.

### Cited Findings
- Peeters and Bizer (arXiv 2305.03423) do not override ChatGPT with a string threshold. They use Jaccard similarity only to pick in-context demonstrations. Related demonstrations improve zero-shot F1 by up to 7.85%. Writing higher-level matching rules into the zero-shot prompt raises F1 by 6 points to 88.29%. Recall stays at 98%; precision rises by nearly 10%. The zero-shot baseline they compare with a fine-tuned RoBERTa is 82.35% F1, a level RoBERTa reaches only with about 2,000 training pairs. The gain comes from instructing the model, not from discarding its decision. — [Using ChatGPT for Entity Matching](https://arxiv.org/html/2305.03423)
- Wang et al. (COLING 2025, arXiv 2405.16884) stack language models, not a string veto. On eight ER datasets, a comparing prompt beats independent matching by 5.24% F1 on average, and a selecting prompt gains up to 8.15% F1 over comparing. Across the study, selecting improves on the matching strategy by 13.39% F1 on average. ComEM then uses a smaller model to filter candidates and a larger model to select, which adds up to 8.46% F1 over selecting alone and cuts cost. An appendix remark says one could vary how many candidates are kept using similarity scores. That use is unmeasured in the paper. — [arXiv 2405.16884](https://arxiv.org/html/2405.16884v1)
- Mudgal et al. (SIGMOD 2018) compare deep matchers with Magellan, whose features are classical string similarities. Deep models do not beat Magellan on structured entity matching; they do beat it on textual and dirty matching. That is “do not replace the string learner on structured fields,” not “overrule a language model that already said non-match.” No numeric F1 was re-read for this note. — [Mudgal et al. 2018](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3183713.3196926)
- Zhang et al. (arXiv 2607.24688, 27 July 2026) never combine a string score with the language-model decision. On a shift toward same-title negatives, larger models over-predict matches. Cross-encoder F1 falls from 97.6 at 0.6B to 85.0 at 8B, and precision falls from 96.9 to 76.4, while recall stays near 99. Generative-matcher F1 falls from 94.0 to 90.0 and precision from 90.9 to 83.7. Scaling makes the false-match problem worse. This is the opposite of a high-similarity veto: the large model trusts surface equality too much. — [arXiv 2607.24688](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2607.24688)
- A 19 September 2026 Towards Data Science demo on vendor strings, not addresses, sweeps a fuzzy score. Precision is 0.345 at 85, 0.764 at 90, 0.895 at 95 (recall already 0.738), and 1.0 only at 98 (recall 0.485). Every pair in that perfect band is the same name under two country domains (brand.com vs brand.de). The author treats the score as a review rank, not an auto-merge. This is a blog demonstration, not a peer-reviewed address experiment. — [Towards Data Science, 19 Sep 2026](https://towardsdatascience.com/one-vendor-four-spellings-how-deterministic-stages-beat-similarity-scores/)
- Classical pipelines do auto-accept a string rule before any second model, but the second model is not a language model and the precision of the accept is often unpublished. Census BITS removes a pair from later passes once an exact or standardized name matches, on the prior that those passes are cleaner. McCallum canopy clustering and the 2025 in-context clustering paper use a high similarity threshold to form blocks, not to overturn a matcher. — [Chow et al. 2021](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w28839/w28839.pdf); [arXiv 2506.02509](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.02509)

### Inferences
- The published pattern closest to “trust the string model in one band and the language model in another” is blocking plus a matcher, or Jaccard-selected prompts. Neither paper reports a test where the string score wins a head-to-head against a language-model non-match. Claiming that override as a known win would be unmeasured.
- Zhang et al. 2026 are evidence against a blanket “high string similarity means match” rule once negatives include lookalike titles, and against “use a larger model to correct them”: on that shift the larger cross-encoder’s precision fell by about 20 points. Their shift is same-title negatives, not a one-token descriptor swap, so it does not by itself decide this team’s pattern.
- If a rule is to be introduced, the only measured quality gain of that kind is Peeters and Bizer’s prompt rule (+6 F1, precision up nearly 10 points, recall unchanged at 98%). That still lets the language model decide.

### Gaps
- No retrieved experiment reports F1, precision, or recall for the policy “if string similarity ≥ t, ignore the language model.” The Towards Data Science thresholds are a blog sweep on vendor strings.
- ComEM’s suggestion to cut the candidate list with similarity is explicitly unmeasured.
- Mudgal et al.’s structured-data F1 numbers were not re-opened; only the qualitative result is cited.

## Does a cross-encoder fine-tuned on in-domain pairs get worse on an unseen language, specifically on one-token substitutions, and what fixed it?

### Takeaway
No retrieved paper measures one-token substitutions on an unseen language. The nearest measured facts are: an English Transformer fine-tuned only on German product pairs falls behind a linear SVM, and mixing English pairs plus a multilingual encoder removes that gap; separately, a Qwen3 cross-encoder loses recall on unseen attribute combinations and loses precision when scaled on same-title negatives, and rebalancing the training mix is what the authors show fixes the recall collapse. Character models, and Qwen itself, are not the fix in those studies.

### Cited Findings
- Peeters and Bizer (arXiv 2110.03338; WebConf 2022 poster) fine-tune Transformer product matchers on 1,800 German pairs, with and without 7,200 extra English pairs. F1 without English pairs: linear SVM 71.00, English BERT 65.27, German BERT 73.43, mBERT 87.69, XLM-R 73.40. F1 with the English pairs added: SVM 71.05, English BERT 74.29, German BERT 89.83, mBERT 91.44, XLM-R 86.98. The prose states that English BERT on German-only fine-tuning is about 6 F1 behind the SVM because of the pretraining/fine-tuning language mismatch, that mBERT is best on German-only data (about 87 F1, 14 points above German BERT), and that adding English pairs gives German BERT its largest gain (about 16 F1). The task is product-offer matching across shops. The paper does not slice errors by one-token substitution, and it does not evaluate a held-out third language with no target-language pairs. — [arXiv 2110.03338 PDF](http://arxiv.org/pdf/2110.03338v1)
- Zhang, Li, Calixto, Groth, and Schelter (arXiv 2607.24688, 27 July 2026) run 1,215 fine-tunes of Qwen3 (0.6B, 4B, 8B; base, instruction-tuned, and embedding variants) as bi-encoders, cross-encoders, and generative matchers on nine Magellan, MaChAmp, and WDC product, publication, book, movie, and software datasets. A full-text search of the PDF finds no breakdown by edit type, token substitution, character n-gram, or language. What they do measure: on a split where 82% of positive test pairs have unseen attribute combinations, cross-encoder recall stays low at 42.6, 48.9, and 51.1 across the three sizes, while the 8B generative matcher reaches recall 84.8 at high precision. Zero-shot GPT on that split scores F1 94.0 against Ditto at 56.3, mostly a recall gap. After they rebalance so that unseen attribute combinations are 17% rather than 82%, cross-encoder recall rises to about 96–97 at every size and F1 matches the generative matcher. On a different shift, more same-title negatives, cross-encoder F1 falls from 97.6 (0.6B) to 85.0 (8B) and precision from 96.9 to 76.4, with recall still near 99. Generative F1 falls from 94.0 to 90.0 and precision from 90.9 to 83.7. They attribute the scaling loss to shortcut learning. Bi-encoders transfer worst across datasets; generative matchers retain the most target-dataset performance; cross-encoders sit in between and are the better accuracy/throughput choice when the labeled data match deployment. — [arXiv 2607.24688](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2607.24688)
- Steiner, Peeters, and Bizer (arXiv 2409.08185, revised May 2025) find that fine-tuning LLMs for entity matching improves generalization to other in-domain datasets and hurts cross-domain transfer. Smaller models gain more from fine-tuning; larger models are mixed. Adding structured explanations helps three of four LLMs. Their example-selection and example-generation methods help only Llama 3.1 8B and hurt GPT-4o-mini. No language split and no one-token split. — [arXiv 2409.08185](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.08185)
- Peeters and Bizer’s JointBERT result (dual-objective fine-tune) raises F1 on seen products by 1 to 5 points when both objectives have enough labels, and underperforms other methods on unseen products. Again not a language or edit-type slice. — [PVLDB abstract](http://vldb.org/pvldb/volumes/14/paper/Dual-Objective%20Fine-Tuning%20of%20BERT%20for%20Entity%20Matching)
- Character-level name models are measured on retrieval, not on this match pattern. A 2026 byte-level encoder trained from scratch on UTF-8, with no pretrained vocabulary, reports 0.775 MRR and 0.897 recall at 10 for cross-script phonetic person-name retrieval over eight non-Latin scripts, and a 10× smaller Latin/non-Latin gap than the best classical baseline in that post. The author states that dense semantic retrievers degrade when the query is transliterated, because a two-token name has no sentence context (citing Chari et al. 2025). That is not a test of Collège versus Pharmacie, which are different words rather than transliterations. — [Towards Data Science, 26 Apr 2026](https://towardsdatascience.com/bytes-speak-all-languages-cross-script-name-retrieval-via-contrastive-learning/)

### Inferences
- The failure mode that is actually measured for fine-tuned pair encoders is distribution shift in the training mix, not the orthography of one token. English-only pretraining plus a small target-language fine-tune loses to a linear SVM (65.27 vs 71.00 F1). mBERT, already multilingual, is the model that does not need the extra English pairs to get into the high 80s. XLM-R on this one poster does need the mix: 73.40 without English pairs, 86.98 with them. Copying “use XLM-R” without the mix is not what the table shows.
- Zhang et al. give the analogous fix for a cross-encoder that has learned the wrong shortcut: put the missing case into the fine-tuning distribution. Recall went from the mid-40s/low-50s to about 96–97 when unseen attribute combinations were reduced from 82% to 17%. Making the cross-encoder larger did not fix it, and on same-title negatives it lowered precision. If an in-country judge has learned “any content-token substitution is a non-match,” the paper’s evidence says to rebalance those pairs in training, not to scale the same objective. That extrapolation to descriptor tokens is unmeasured. Their datasets are products and citations, not street addresses, and not a leave-one-country language split.
- Nothing retrieved says a character n-gram model repairs a cross-encoder’s one-token errors on a new language. Jimenez et al. show character bigrams are the weaker name matcher on business strings (IAP 0.529 vs SoftTF-IDF 0.704) and the stronger one on census typos. A character model will not treat Collège and Pharmacie as equivalent descriptors; it will treat them as a large edit. Using it as the fix for false non-matches on descriptor swaps is unmeasured and, on that evidence, the wrong direction. It remains the right direction for misspellings of the same token.

### Gaps
- No paper opened here, including arXiv 2607.24688, reports precision or recall of a fine-tuned cross-encoder on one-token substitutions in a language absent from fine-tuning. The Qwen3 study was checked in the PDF for edit type, token substitution, and language; those cuts are absent. The authors themselves ask future benchmarks to report subgroup errors rather than only aggregate F1.
- Peeters and Bizer 2021 never test a language with zero target-language pairs. German fine-tuning data are always present (1,800 pairs). It is not a leave-one-country result.
- No result here shows that Qwen, mBERT, XLM-R, or a character model specifically repairs descriptor-level substitutions. mBERT and mixed-language training repair overall German product-match F1. Rebalancing repairs Qwen3 cross-encoder recall under an attribute-combination shift.
- The byte-level MRR figures are person-name retrieval across scripts, from a blog write-up of the authors’ own system, not an entity-matching benchmark.
