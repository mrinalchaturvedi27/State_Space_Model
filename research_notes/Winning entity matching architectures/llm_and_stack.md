# Retrieval, gradient-boosted trees, and language-model judges for entity resolution (2023–2026)

Scope note: “Dirty ER” is used in two senses in this literature. Magellan-style papers mean noisy or textual attributes. LLM-CER means deduplication of one dirty collection, as opposed to clean–clean linkage. Business-name and address matching at million-record scale is rarely the benchmark these LLM papers actually run. Most F1 numbers below are on product or bibliographic pair benchmarks, often down-sampled to about 1,250 pairs. Where a claim was not measured in the sources opened for this pass, it is marked unmeasured.

The pipeline being contrasted is: bi-encoder blocking, a first LightGBM on string and rival features, a LoRA pairwise judge of about 4B parameters whose score is a feature in a second LightGBM stacker, then a precision-heavy decision rule.

## How do MatchGPT, AnyMatch, Unicorn, FineMatch, BatchER, and similar systems use the LLM, and how do they score against a BERT-style cross-encoder and a GBDT?

### Takeaway
In the 2023–2026 papers that were opened, the LLM (or the small LM standing in for it) is almost always the final pairwise or listwise classifier, sometimes after a cheaper filter, and sometimes also a labeler or explanation generator. It is not used as a numeric feature inside a gradient-boosted tree. No head-to-head F1 of these LLM matchers against LightGBM or another GBDT was found. Against in-domain RoBERTa or Ditto, zero-shot GPT-4 is close and sometimes a few points higher on products, and a few points lower on bibliographic sets; the same PLMs collapse by tens of F1 points on unseen products. A fine-tuned 124M GPT-2 (AnyMatch) is the cost-efficient zero-shot transfer winner among open models in that study, not a prompted 7B–70B model.

### Cited Findings
- MatchGPT (Peeters, Steiner, Bizer; arXiv:2310.11244v4, EDBT 2025) passes a serialized pair plus a yes/no question to a generative LLM and parses the word “yes”. The LLM is the final classifier in zero-shot, few-shot, rule-augmented, and fine-tuned settings. GPT-4 is also used later to write structured explanations and to cluster wrong decisions into error classes. That is analysis, not a feature for another model. Open models in the main zero-shot table are Llama-2-70B-chat, Llama-3.1-70B-Instruct, and Mixtral-8x7B-Instruct-v0.1. Hosted models are gpt-4-0613, gpt-4o-2024-08-06, and gpt-4o-mini-2024-07-18. Test sets are down-sampled to about 1,250 pairs. — [MatchGPT HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2310.11244v4)

- Earlier single-benchmark ChatGPT study (Peeters and Bizer, arXiv:2305.03423, ADBIS 2023): zero-shot ChatGPT 82.35 F1 on one hard matching task; RoBERTa fine-tuned on 2,000 pairs 82.72 F1; RoBERTa on the original ~20,000 pairs 89.32 F1. - AnyMatch (Zhang, Groth, Calixto, Schelter; arXiv:2409.04073, 2024) fine-tunes GPT-2 (124M, decoder-only) as a sequence classifier on other labeled datasets, then applies it with no target labels and no column names. The LM is the final classifier. An AutoML model is used only while building the fine-tune set: positives that the AutoML model misclassifies are kept as difficult examples, attribute-level pair examples are added, and negatives are capped at twice the positives. The AutoML model is not in the inference path and is not identified as LightGBM in the sections read. Mean F1 on nine datasets (Abt-Buy, Amazon-Google, Beer, DBLP-ACM, DBLP-GoogleScholar, Fodors-Zagats, iTunes-Amazon, Walmart-Amazon, WDC), zero-shot transfer protocol: AnyMatch 81.96; MatchGPT GPT-4 86.36; MatchGPT GPT-4o-mini 81.01; Unicorn 77.54; Jellyfish-13B 77.83 but with several datasets in parentheses as seen in training; ZeroER 66.86; Ditto (110M, transfer, not in-domain) 66.05; MatchGPT Mixtral-8x7B 64.26; string similarity 40.21. Authors: within 4.4% average F1 of GPT-4, four orders of magnitude fewer parameters (124M vs 1.76T as they count GPT-4), 3,899× lower dollars per 1,000 tokens. Per-dataset F1, same column order, MatchGPT GPT-4: 94.40, 74.91, 69.57, 95.60, 87.22, 97.67, 82.35, 89.67, 85.83. AnyMatch: 86.05, 55.08, 96.55, 93.61, 90.59, 100.00, 90.91, 61.51, 63.31. AnyMatch is weak on Walmart-Amazon (61.51) and WDC (63.31) relative to GPT-4 (89.67 and 85.83). — [AnyMatch HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.04073v2); [AnyMatch PDF mirror](https://openreview.net/pdf?id=Nees1OD5td)
- Unicorn (Fan, Tu, Li, et al.; ACM pdf 10.1145/3665252.3665263) is a unified multi-task matcher: one encoder for any pair of data elements, a mixture-of-experts layer, and a binary matcher. It is a PLM-scale model (145M in the AnyMatch table), not a generative LLM judge, and the matcher head is the final classifier. Zero-shot transfer mean F1 77.54 in AnyMatch’s table, below AnyMatch (81.96) and GPT-4 (86.36), above transfer Ditto (66.05). Per-dataset from that table: Abt-Buy 89.45, Amazon-Google 55.70, Beer 90.32, DBLP-ACM 94.33, DBLP-GoogleScholar 94.11, Fodors-Zagats 90.91, iTunes-Amazon 72.22, Walmart-Amazon 63.57, WDC 47.28. — [Unicorn PDF](https://dl.acm.org/doi/pdf/10.1145/3665252.3665263); [AnyMatch table](https://openreview.net/pdf?id=Nees1OD5td)
- No paper titled FineMatch was found. The closest named work is Steiner, Peeters, and Bizer, “Fine-tuning Large Language Models for Entity Matching” (arXiv:2409.08185, 12 Sep 2024; slides at DAIS @ ICDE 2025). The LLM is again the final classifier. Models: GPT-4o, GPT-4o-mini, Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct, Llama-3.1-70B-Instruct. On standard fine-tuning, Llama-3.1-8B gains 17.31 F1 on average over its zero-shot baseline; Abt-Buy moves from 56.57 to 87.34 (+30.77); WDC from 53.36 to 69.19 (+15.83). GPT-4o-mini gains 11.72 F1 on average; WDC from 81.61 to 84.38 (+2.77). Llama-3.1-70B fine-tuned on WDC falls from 75.19 to 72.66 (−2.53) on WDC. GPT-4o on WDC rises from 81.64 to 87.07 (+5.43). Product-to-scholar transfer: Llama-8B models drop 11.05 F1 on average versus their own zero-shot; GPT-4o-mini drops 2.07 F1. Authors: fine-tuning helps in-domain transfer and hurts cross-domain transfer in the majority of cases. Whether the Llama runs used LoRA is not stated in the sections read. — [Fine-tuning LLMs for EM](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.08185v1)
- BatchER (Fan et al., arXiv:2312.03987, Dec 2023) keeps the LLM as the final classifier but batches many unlabeled pairs into one in-context prompt, with a covering-based demo selector. SBERT or RoBERTa is an embedding function for choosing demonstrations, not the matcher. Authors’ summary: batch prompting gives a 4×–7× cost reduction and higher, more stable accuracy than standard prompting; the best design is diversity-based question batching plus covering-based demonstration selection; they claim this is more cost-effective than Ditto, JointBERT, and RobEM fine-tuned on many labels, and than hand-written LLM prompts. The per-dataset F1 table (their Table III) was not extracted in this pass, so no numeric F1 versus Ditto is reported here. Models discussed: GPT-3.5-turbo, GPT-4-1106-preview, Llama-2-chat-70B. A worked cost example: 1,000 records, ~500,000 pair questions, ~90 tokens per pair, 3 demos, GPT-4 at $0.01/1K input tokens ≈ $1,800. — [BatchER HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2312.03987v1)
- ComEM (Wang et al., arXiv:2405.16884v3, COLING 2025) is a cascade of LLMs, all used as classifiers. After blocking, a medium local LLM ranks or filters candidates with pairwise matching or comparing; a stronger LLM then selects the match from the top-k list. Final decision is the selector LLM, not a GBDT. Selecting beats pairwise matching by 16.02% average F1 on 8 ER datasets (the May 2024 v1 abstract said 13.39%; use the v3 figure). ComEM then adds up to 4% F1 over selecting alone and reduces cost. Ten LLMs were tried. Position bias: selection quality changes as the true match moves down the list. — [ComEM v3 HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2405.16884v3); [v1 abstract](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.16884)
- Jellyfish (Zhang et al., arXiv:2312.01678) instruction-tunes Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.2, Llama-3-8B-Instruct, and a 13B model as a general data-preprocessing model. Entity matching is one of four tasks, and the tuned LLM is the classifier. AnyMatch’s zero-shot table lists Jellyfish at 13B parameters with mean F1 77.83, with parentheses marking datasets that were seen in training, so it is not a clean zero-shot number. The Hugging Face card for Jellyfish-8B is CC-BY-NC-4.0. A long F1 table is on that card, but the column headers were not recovered cleanly here, so those per-column scores are not assigned to models. — [Jellyfish HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2312.01678); [Jellyfish-8B card](https://huggingface.co/NECOUDBFM/Jellyfish-8B); [AnyMatch table](https://openreview.net/pdf?id=Nees1OD5td)
- ChatMatcher (Information Systems, Vol. 142, Dec 2026, article 102784; best of DOLAP 2025) is an end-to-end pipeline: any filtering method, then a 7B LLM in 4-bit as the matcher (zero- or few-shot), then bipartite clustering for clean–clean or dirty ER. The LLM is the verifier, not a feature. Models named: Gemma-3n, Llama-3.1, Qwen2.5. The abstract says it beats ComEM. No F1 number is in the accessible abstract (paywalled). — [ChatMatcher](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0306437926000980)
- “On Leveraging Large Language Models for Enhancing Entity Resolution” (Li et al., arXiv:2401.03426, ICDE 2024) uses an LLM to answer a budgeted subset of matching questions (MQsSP, stated NP-hard) and then updates partitions. The LLM is a selective oracle, not a feature vector and not a classifier of every pair. No F1 was extracted. — [arXiv:2401.03426](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.03426)
- LLM-CER (arXiv:2506.02509) is clustering-based dirty ER. The LLM decides cluster merges, with checks aimed at hallucinations. Reported gains versus baselines: up to 150% higher accuracy, 10% higher FP-measure, up to 5× fewer API calls, on nine datasets. The LLM is a cluster-level judge, not a pairwise GBDT feature. — [LLM-CER](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.02509)
- A July 2026 preprint (arXiv:2607.26298v1) does compare LightGBM to neural matchers, but the LLM is a teacher that labels pairs, not a feature. Under a cap of 10K training records, a tournament among DeepMatcher, LightGBM on attribute features, and a GAT picks a winner per dataset. Pair-F1 (parentheses look like a second metric in the extract, not re-interpreted here): Restaurants DeepMatcher 0.948 / LightGBM 0.969 / GAT 0.748 (LightGBM); Cora 0.968 / 0.891 / 0.809 (DeepMatcher); Geo Settlements 0.979 / 0.960 / 0.964 (DeepMatcher); DBLP-Scholar 0.160 / 0.541 / 0.239 (LightGBM); MB 200K 0.964 / 0.948 / 0.833 (DeepMatcher); NCV 0.992 / 0.993 / 0.989 (LightGBM, margin < 0.001). Each family wins 3 of 6; GAT wins none. Teacher cost about $450 per 1M pairs at about 2 seconds per pair; the winning student about $12 per 1M pairs (37.5×). LightGBM winners 222–263K pairs/sec on CPU; DeepMatcher winners 5–10K pairs/sec including SBERT. The paper’s line is “no single matcher wins; let them compete,” not “stack them.” — [arXiv:2607.26298v1](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2607.26298v1)
- Ditto as a cross-encoder baseline, from the VLDB Journal 2023 paper (not the MatchGPT re-run): a straightforward BERT, DistilBERT, or RoBERTa sequence-pair classifier already beats prior SOTA by up to 29% F1; domain knowledge, summarization, and hard-example augmentation add up to 9.8% F1; Ditto can match prior SOTA with at most half the labels; on two company files of 789K and 412K records it reports F1 96.5%. That company result is a cross-encoder final classifier on a large but not fully described name/address task. — [Effective entity matching with transformers](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00778-023-00779-z)
- “Beyond Scale and Generation” (Zhang et al., arXiv:2607.24688, 28 Jul 2026) factorially fine-tunes Qwen3 at 0.6B, 4B, and 8B, in base, instruction, and embedding variants, as bi-encoders, cross-encoders, and generative matchers: 1,215 runs, nine datasets. Qualitative results, with per-cell F1 not extracted: cross-encoders stay ahead of bi-encoders because they jointly encode the pair; size narrows that gap but does not remove it; generative matchers do not always beat cross-encoders, and their edge shows up under schema shift and cross-dataset transfer; larger models lean more on shortcut learning and are not automatically better. Bi-encoders are described as the usual blocking tool because embeddings can be cached. LoRA versus full fine-tuning is not stated in the extract. — [arXiv:2607.24688](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2607.24688)

### Inferences
- Sketch that these papers actually support: (1) block or otherwise propose candidates; (2) optionally rank them with a cheap model (rules, a medium LLM, or a learned hash); (3) let one language model emit the match decision, either as yes/no, as a choice from a short list, or as a fine-tuned sequence label; (4) optionally cluster. The language-model score is not fed to a second tree.
- For a ≤8B, locally runnable matcher, the published quality/cost point closest to “small model beats prompted giants” is AnyMatch’s 124M GPT-2 at mean F1 81.96 versus prompted Mixtral-8x7B at 64.26 and versus GPT-4 at 86.36, on transfer benchmarks, not on million-row business addresses. Llama-3.1-8B is a different point: weak zero-shot (WDC 53.36) and much stronger after in-domain fine-tuning (69.19), still below GPT-4o-mini’s fine-tuned 84.38 on that set.
- In-domain Ditto/RoBERTa in MatchGPT must not be compared to transfer Ditto in AnyMatch (66.05 mean). They are different protocols.
- A GBDT comparison for MatchGPT, AnyMatch, Unicorn, BatchER, and ComEM is unmeasured. The 2026 tournament shows LightGBM beating DeepMatcher on some sets and losing on others when both are trained as the matcher, which cuts against “always use the neural model” and also against “always use the tree.”

### Gaps
- FineMatch: no paper under that name was found.
- Numeric F1 for BatchER versus Ditto, for ChatMatcher versus ComEM, and for the ICDE 2024 budgeted-question LLM method: not extracted (table not reached, or paywalled).
- AutoML implementation inside AnyMatch (which library, whether a GBDT): not identified.
- LoRA versus full fine-tuning in Steiner et al. and in the Qwen3 factorial study: not stated in the pages read.
- No source in this pass reports LightGBM or XGBoost F1 on the Magellan/WDC pair sets beside GPT-4 or Ditto.

## Does a published stacker (GBDT on an LM score plus symbolic features) beat the LM alone on dirty ER?

### Takeaway
No. This pass did not find a peer-reviewed result in which a gradient-boosted tree that takes an LM pairwise score plus symbolic features beats that LM alone on dirty entity resolution. The near misses use the LM as a labeler, use an embedding distance as one tree feature without comparing to an LM judge, or use AutoML only to pick training rows.

### Cited Findings
- The 2026 tournament (arXiv:2607.26298v1) explicitly runs LightGBM and DeepMatcher as competitors, not as a stack. LightGBM’s inputs are attribute features; DeepMatcher’s are embeddings plus attributes. The LLM labels training data and is not a feature at inference. On DBLP-Scholar, the set where a neural model might be expected to dominate noisy text, LightGBM Pair-F1 is 0.541 versus DeepMatcher 0.160 under the ≤10K-label cap. On Cora, DeepMatcher is 0.968 versus LightGBM 0.891. That is evidence that the winner flips, not that a stacker wins. — [arXiv:2607.26298v1](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2607.26298v1)
- AnyMatch’s AutoML filter selects difficult positive training pairs for GPT-2. At inference only GPT-2 scores the pair. There is no second-stage tree, and no ablation of “GPT-2 score plus string features in a GBDT” versus GPT-2. — [AnyMatch](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.04073v2)
- An engineering name-matching repository trains LightGBM on eight similarities, one of which is the embedding distance from `all-MiniLM-L6-v2`, plus Jaccard, TF-IDF cosine, and several fuzzy ratios. That is an embedding feature inside a tree, not a pairwise LM-judge score, and the repository does not report F1 against an LM classifier. Unmeasured as a stacker result. — [vietexob/name-matching](https://github.com/vietexob/name-matching)
- A 2026 challenge write-up (not a paper) matches noisy business records with blocking plus one LightGBM and no LM score: validation macro F0.5 0.8899 at threshold 0.650 on 40,000 held-out entities, leaderboard 0.875, micro precision 0.9718, micro recall 0.8013. It does not test whether adding an LM score would help. — [Amazon-ML-Challenge](https://github.com/DevanshSrajput/Amazon-ML-Challenge)
- ComEM uses the medium LLM’s ranking only to choose which pairs the large LLM sees. The large LLM’s decision is final. There is no learned stacker on top of both. — [ComEM](https://arxiv.org/html/2405.16884v3)
- Zeakis et al., VLDB Journal 2025, compare 12 pretrained embedding models for blocking, unsupervised matching, and supervised matching on 17 datasets, schema-agnostic and schema-aware, and discuss complementarity. The abstract does not describe a GBDT stacked on a cross-encoder score, and the winning model’s F1 was not extracted (paywalled full text). — [VLDB Journal 2025 embeddings study](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00778-024-00879-4)

### Inferences
- The stacker in the proposed pipeline (LightGBM features include a ~4B LoRA pairwise score, then a precision rule) is a design that practitioners might expect to work, because trees handle exact identifiers and LMs handle paraphrase, but it is not a result these papers report. Calling it best would be unmeasured.
- The closest supported alternative is distillation: pay for the LLM once as a labeler, deploy LightGBM or a small neural matcher, and accept that which student wins is dataset-dependent (3/6 each for LightGBM and DeepMatcher in the 2026 tournament).
- If a stacker result exists, it was not in MatchGPT, AnyMatch, Unicorn, BatchER, ComEM, Jellyfish, the Steiner fine-tuning paper, or the 2026 tournament under the queries used here.

### Gaps
- No citation was found for “GBDT on LM score + symbolic features > LM alone” on dirty ER, WDC, or business-name/address data. This is a real gap, not a negative experimental result.
- Complementarity numbers from Zeakis et al. (whether concatenating several embedders beats the best one) were not read.

## Which open models at or under 8B parameters are repeatedly used for matching text as of 2026, and what are their licenses?

### Takeaway
As of 26 Sep 2026, the open generative models that show up repeatedly inside entity-matching papers at or under 8B are Llama-3 / Llama-3.1 8B, Qwen2.5 (ChatMatcher’s 7B setting), Qwen3 at 0.6B/4B/8B, Mistral-7B as Jellyfish’s base, and GPT-2 124M in AnyMatch. Only the Qwen2.5-7B and Qwen3-4B cards opened here are Apache-2.0. Llama 3.1 is not Apache or MIT. E5 and BGE are repeatedly used in 2025 retrieval training papers, not as the pairwise ER judge in the EM papers read here. Qwen2.5-3B is the wrong license for an Apache/MIT constraint even though it is under 8B.

(May 2025, revised Oct 2025) study the BGE training collection (1.6M query–passage pairs, 15 datasets) and report gains for E5-base and for a Qwen2.5-7B retriever after false-negative relabeling: +0.7 to +1.4 nDCG@10 on BEIR, and +1.7 to +1.8 nDCG@10 on zero-shot AIR-Bench; a Qwen2.5-3B reranker fine-tuned on the relabeled data also improves on BEIR. That is retrieval, not entity matching. Zeakis et al. (VLDBJ 2025) say fastText and BERT variants have been the popular embedding models for ER blocking and matching; they evaluate 12 PLMs on 17 ER datasets. The list of 12 was not extracted, so it is unmeasured whether E5 or BGE won that study. TriBERTa (arXiv:2411.10629, Nov 2024) fine-tunes SBERT with a triplet loss for ER and reports a 3–19% margin over untuned SBERT and TF-IDF; the abstract does not name E5 or BGE. — [Thakur et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.16967); [Zeakis et al.](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00778-024-00879-4); [TriBERTa](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.10629)
- MatchGPT’s stronger open zero-shot models are above 8B: Llama-3.1-70B (best open zero-shot average in that paper, 84.87 F1 on domain-complex-force) and Mixtral-8x7B (68.29 on the same prompt). They do not satisfy the size cap. — [MatchGPT](https://arxiv.org/html/2310.11244v4)

### Inferences
- Under an Apache-2.0 or MIT and ≤8B constraint, the model family these 2025–2026 matching papers actually fine-tune, and whose license was verified on a model card, is Qwen: Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct (Apache-2.0, 7.61B) and Qwen3-4B / Qwen3-4B-Instruct-2507 (Apache-2.0, 4.0B). Qwen3-4B is the size closest to the proposed ~4B LoRA judge, and the 2026 factorial study is the one that treats that size as a cross-encoder and as a generative matcher. Qwen2.5-3B is under 8B but the vendor’s own table puts it under the Qwen Research License, so it fails the license constraint.
- Llama-3.1-8B is the model ER fine-tuning papers report the most in-domain F1 movement for ( +17.31 average F1, WDC 53.36 → 69.19), but the license cited here is the Llama 3.1 Community License, so it is out of scope if Apache-2.0 or MIT is mandatory. Jellyfish-8B is additionally CC-BY-NC-4.0.
- E5/BGE should be treated as blocking or retrieval embedders with a strong IR evidence base, not as models repeatedly shown to be the best pairwise ER judges in this pass. Their MIT/Apache status was not re-read from cards here.

### Gaps
- Official Hugging Face license text was not opened for Llama-3.1-8B, Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.2, Gemma-3n, GPT-2, BAAI/bge-m3, intfloat E5, or Qwen3-8B. Do not treat those licenses as confirmed except where a card or vendor table was actually fetched (Qwen2.5-7B Apache-2.0, Qwen2.5-3B not Apache, Qwen3-4B Apache-2.0, Jellyfish-8B CC-BY-NC-4.0, Gemma-2 family “Gemma Terms of Use” via the Arena table).
- No count was made of how many ER papers in 2026 use each embedder. “Repeatedly” above means “appeared in more than one of the systems read,” not a systematic census.

## What failure modes are documented when an LM matcher is trained only on hard negatives or on a previous model’s mistakes?

### Takeaway
Training on, or filtering toward, a model’s own errors can raise in-domain F1 for Llama-3.1-8B and still damage cross-domain F1. Dropping the pairs a stronger model got wrong can erase the fine-tuning gain and land at or below zero-shot. Hard-negative mining in retrieval is separately documented to inject false negatives that hurt the embedder. A pure “hard negatives only” ER ablation with a measured F1 drop was not found; the ER papers that emphasize hard pairs still mix them with ordinary negatives or with the original training set.

### Cited Findings
- Steiner et al. define error-based filtering as prompting GPT-4o-mini on the WDC-small training split (2,500 pairs) and discarding pairs whose predicted label disagrees with the gold label. The kept set has 2,006 pairs. Relevancy filtering asks GPT-4o for “interesting” pairs; the model keeps highly similar corner cases, leaving 608 pairs. WDC Products itself is the “most challenging version, with 80% corner cases (hard positives and negatives).” — [Fine-tuning paper](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.08185v1)
- On Llama-3.1-8B, deltas versus fine-tuning on unfiltered WDC-small (Table 5): error-based filter (drop the teacher’s mistakes) WDC F1 73.92 (+4.73); relevancy filter 72.37 (+3.18); synthetic examples plus filter 72.54 (+3.35); synthetic plus relevancy 74.04 (+4.85). Both filtered sets beat Llama-8B trained on the large WDC set of nearly 20,000 examples. In-domain transfer capture is 75% and 76% of dedicated-model performance versus 72% for the unfiltered fine-tune. — [Fine-tuning paper](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.08185v1)
- The same filters hurt GPT-4o-mini. Versus its WDC-small fine-tune: WDC-s-filter 77.06 (−6.25), and the zero-shot row in that table is 77.44, so the filtered model is about at or below zero-shot. In-domain transfer gain for that filter is −61%. Synthetic-plus-filter is 76.89 (−6.42) and also under the zero-shot line. Authors: example selection and generation improve Llama-3.1-8B and decrease GPT-4o-mini. — [Fine-tuning paper](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.08185v1)
- Error-based example selection (train on the previous model’s mistakes, not by deleting them): fine-tune Llama-8B on 2,500 WDC-small pairs, find remaining errors, add 2,500 large-split pairs chosen by embedding similarity to those errors, retrain 5 epochs, repeat five times, keep the best validation checkpoint. Result: WDC F1 74.37 (+5.18 versus standard WDC-small fine-tune), the highest Llama-8B in-domain number in that table, and +1.99 versus training on the large WDC set. Cross-domain columns fall: DBLP-ACM 61.99 (−12.53 versus standard fine-tune) and DBLP-Scholar 55.32 (−12.08), and the cross-domain transfer summary is −97%. In-domain transfer capture is 83% of dedicated performance, better than the 72% baseline, so the damage called out in the paper is cross-domain, not in-domain. — [Fine-tuning paper](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.08185v1)
- Standard fine-tuning, even on the ordinary WDC mix of 80% corner cases, already hurts cross-domain scores: Llama-8B product-to-scholar average −11.05 F1 versus zero-shot, and all eight Llama-8B fine-tunes underperform zero-shot on the scholar sets. Llama-70B fine-tuned on WDC loses 2.53 F1 on WDC itself (75.19 → 72.66) and loses 10.60 and 5.62 F1 on the two scholar sets versus its zero-shot. — [Fine-tuning paper](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.08185v1)
- Generated “matches” were often wrong. Manual inspection: the model emits matching pairs that are easy non-matches (laptops from different product lines labeled as matches). Correctness stays mixed even with a more detailed prompt. That is a label-noise failure mode when the previous model, or the same LLM, synthesizes hard training data. — [Fine-tuning paper](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.08185v1)
- Prompt sensitivity moves the other way: Llama-8B’s F1 standard deviation across prompts falls from 15.76 zero-shot to 1.87 after in-domain fine-tuning, but the fine-tune prompt is not the best prompt in 29 of 42 Llama scenarios. Fine-tuning stabilizes wording; it does not guarantee the training prompt stays optimal. — [Fine-tuning paper](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.08185v1)
- AnyMatch’s stated reason for not training on the raw candidate distribution: blocked candidate sets are heavily negative, and using them all “could lead to serious overfitting (e.g., via instance memorisation) on the minority class.” They keep a 2:1 negative-to-positive ratio and add only the positives a linear/AutoML model got wrong. They present this as a remedy, not as a measured failure of hard-negative-only training. Ablation F1 for “hard positives only” versus the full recipe was not extracted. — [AnyMatch](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.04073v2)
- A 2025 product-matching paper (ScienceDirect S1474034625004318) rebuilds benchmarks so each query has hard negatives, and says a standard pairwise test of 2,293 samples is about 10.2% positives and about 71.3% non-competitive easy negatives. The training loss uses the top negative scores as hard negatives inside a contrastive margin ranking loss, together with the rest of the candidate list via a softmax. This documents that easy-negative benchmarks overstate pairwise EM, not that hard-negative-only training fails. No F1 for “hard negatives only” versus a mixed set was extracted. — [Contrastive margin ranking EM](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1474034625004318)
- Thakur et al., “Hard Negatives, Hard Lessons” (arXiv:2505.16967, 2025), is information retrieval, not ER. Failure mode: mined hard negatives contain false negatives (relevant passages labeled irrelevant). Dropping 8 of 15 datasets in the BGE collection shrinks the training set 2.35× and raises BEIR nDCG@10 by 1.0 point. Relabeling false negatives with an LLM improves E5-base and Qwen2.5-7B by 0.7–1.4 nDCG@10 on BEIR and by 1.7–1.8 nDCG@10 on zero-shot AIR-Bench. Human annotation supports the LLM’s false-negative labels. The same pattern is reported for rerankers such as Qwen2.5-3B. Transfer this to ER only as an analogy: hard negatives taken from a blocker or from a previous matcher are contaminated with missed true matches, and training on them as negatives teaches the wrong boundary. — [arXiv:2505.16967](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.16967)
- Ditto’s earlier result is the opposite intervention, and it is from 2023: augmenting with difficult examples on top of the original data raises F1 by up to 9.8%. That is hard examples added, not hard examples alone. — [Ditto, VLDB Journal](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00778-023-00779-z)
- The Qwen3 factorial study (2026) reports a related scaling failure, without tying it to hard-negative sampling: under a shift of the class-conditional feature distribution, larger models rely more on shortcut learning, which can reduce precision and F1. The numeric drop was not extracted. Generative matchers help most when the shift is schema or dataset shift, which is the same regime where Steiner et al. find that in-domain fine-tuning hurts. — [arXiv:2607.24688](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2607.24688)

### Inferences
- Documented ER failure pattern, with numbers: specializing Llama-3.1-8B on embedding-neighbors of its own errors is the best in-domain WDC result in that table (74.37 F1, +5.18) and one of the worst cross-domain results (−12.5 and −12.1 F1 versus ordinary fine-tuning on the two scholar sets). If the deployment distribution is the same shop mix as WDC, the paper supports this selection. If the next file is a different domain, the paper says it backfires.
- Documented failure for the stronger model: deleting pairs GPT-4o-mini already misses (the natural reading of “train on what the previous model found easy”) removes its fine-tuning gain (83-ish → 77.06, next to a 77.44 zero-shot).
- A pipeline that mines the first LightGBM’s mistakes and fine-tunes the 4B judge only on those pairs is the err-sel setup. The published risk is not in-domain F1 collapse; it is cross-domain collapse and, by analogy with Thakur, false negatives hiding inside the “hard negative” bucket. Neither paper tests business-name/address data.
- AnyMatch’s practical countermeasure is explicit ratio control (2 negatives per positive) plus hard positives, not hard negatives alone.

### Gaps
- No ER paper in this pass reports an ablation titled “trained only on hard negatives” with precision, recall, and F1 against a mixed-negative control on a dirty business or address set.
- The CMRL paper’s F1 under the hard-negative candidate regime was not extracted.
- Shortcut-learning F1 drops by Qwen3 size in arXiv:2607.24688 were not extracted.
- Whether err-sel’s extra 2,500 pairs are mostly hard negatives, hard positives, or both is not broken out; they are “similar to identified errors.”

## Which candidate-generation patterns are reported to work at million-record scale?

### Takeaway
The pattern that production-oriented systems and the large engineering write-up actually use is a union of several tight indexes (exact or hashed tokens over different field combinations), then a cap or a cheap ranker, then an expensive scorer on a handful of candidates per record. Embedding ANN is a researched alternative and a complement, not the only winner. A learned GBDT as the cheap filter before a 4B judge was not what these sources measured; they either stop at the GBDT or they use a smaller LLM as the filter before a larger LLM.

### Cited Findings
- Splink (UK Ministry of Justice; blocking guide dated in the fetch as 2026-09-03) generates the candidate set as the union of SQL blocking rules, typically about 3 to 10 rules, sometimes more. Each rule is an equality (or a substring equality) on one or more fields, for example `block_on("first_name", "surname")` together with `block_on("postcode")`, or a longer list that mixes name+postcode-prefix, surname+date of birth, first name+date of birth, date of birth+postcode, first name+postcode, and surname+postcode. A pair is compared if it satisfies any rule. One rule cannot be both high-recall and small; the union is the recommended fix. The scorer after blocking is a Fellegi–Sunter model, not a neural judge. — [Splink blocking rules](https://moj-analytical-services.github.io/splink/topic_guides/blocking/blocking_rules.html); [Splink tutorial](https://moj-analytical-services.github.io/splink/demos/tutorials/03_Blocking.html)
- Splink discussion on company name plus address (Feb 2024, maintainer reply Jan 2025): more than 15 blocking rules were used so that name overlap was always required, because a model trained in the default way assigned very high probability (the user wrote 99.9%) to different names at the same address. The maintainer’s diagnosis is a conditional-independence failure: address and postcode match together even for non-matches that share a building, so their weights stack. The recommended fix in Splink is to fuse the correlated fields into one comparison, not to add an LM. This is the most specific published failure mode found for noisy business name and address scoring. — [Splink discussion 1975](https://github.com/moj-analytical-services/splink/discussions/1975)
- A Databricks/Splink workshop example: three rules (full name + date of birth; nationality + locality + premises + region; address line + postcode + surname) reduce 26 billion pairs to about 146,000. That is name/address/id multi-index union on a large comparison space, but not a published million-row business benchmark with F1. — [auto-data-linkage notebook](https://github.com/databricks-industry-solutions/auto-data-linkage/blob/f425125c62904f18f2e2f084467293f1878f908c/Workshop%20Materials/notebooks/Splink%20in%20Databricks%20-%20end-to-end%20example.py)
- Zingg learns a blocking tree: at each node it picks a hash function and a field that drops as few known matching pairs as possible. Documented hash examples include the first N characters of a surname, a Soundex code, the first 3 digits of a postcode, and year of birth. The vendor states that typical comparisons are 0.05–1% of the full space, and that 120k records take about 5 minutes on a 4-core, 10 GB Spark node. The same post’s arithmetic for an 80M-record set (“40,000 to 800,000 comparisons per record”) is 0.05–1% of 80M and should be read as their illustration, not as an independent benchmark. `verifyBlocking` / `debugBlocking` exists because a learned tree can emit pathologically large blocks. A documented failure: rules learned from geographically narrow labels do not transfer; their example is California-only training when the state column is part of blocking and production data has many states. String similarity functions shipped for fuzzy fields include Affine and Jaro. Embedding ANN is not the default in the pages read. — [Zingg blocking at scale](https://www.zingg.ai/post/entity-resolution-at-scale-part-3-blocking); [Zingg hash functions](https://docs.zingg.ai/latest/improving-accuracy/definingown)
- DeepBlocker (VLDB 2021, code still the reference implementation) embeds tuples with a self-supervised autoencoder and retrieves an exact top-K neighbor list per record. The README example uses K=50. It does not need match labels to block. HyperBlocker (2024) compares rule blocking on GPU with DeepBlocker and, in the extracted table, lists DeepBlocker recall as 100% on Fodors-Zagats, 98% on DBLP-Scholar, and 98% on DBLP-ACM, with candidate-set size ratios 15.1, 2.3, and 2.2 (units given as per-myriad in the extract). HyperBlocker’s protocol for that row is top-K with K=5, except K=150 on DBLP-Scholar. These are small benchmarks, not million-row business files. The bracketed “+0 / +5 / +4” were not interpreted, because the prose says they are a deterioration factor relative to HyperBlocker and the sign is ambiguous without the full table. — [DeepBlocker repo](https://github.com/qcri/DeepBlocker); [HyperBlocker PDF extract](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/384698813_HyperBlocker_Accelerating_Rule-based_Blocking_in_Entity_Resolution_using_GPUs)
- Zeakis et al. (VLDB Journal, 4 Dec 2024) re-evaluate 12 pretrained language models for blocking and matching on 17 datasets, using FAISS HNSW for nearest-neighbor blocking, in both schema-agnostic (all attributes concatenated) and schema-aware setups. They study complementarity and transfer. Which embedder won, and at what recall/candidate-size, was not in the abstract. — [Zeakis et al.](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00778-024-00879-4)
- The 2026 business-record challenge (engineering, not peer-reviewed) is the closest million-scale name-matching sketch: about 1.7M source-1 records against about 10M records from three sources, 12M noisy business records in the repository summary, no shared ids. Brute force is given as 2.3×10^13 pairs. Four blocking strategies are unioned, deduplicated, ranked, and truncated to 10 candidates per source-1 entity, leaving 9.65 candidates per entity and 1.67×10^7 scored pairs (about 1.4×10^6 times fewer comparisons). Blocking recall ceiling 0.9415. A single LightGBM on 25 pairwise string features then scores those candidates. Decision rule: threshold τ = 0.650, plus a near one-to-one constraint (99.81% of matched records belong to exactly one source-1 entity). Validation macro F0.5 0.8899 on 40,000 entities; the model captures 94.5% of the blocking-recall ceiling; micro precision 0.9718 and micro recall 0.8013; leaderboard 0.875; trivial predict-nothing baseline 0.0558. Normalization includes 8-script Indic transliteration. No phone/house-number index and no embedding ANN are named in the summary that was read; the four strategies are not itemized there. — [Amazon-ML-Challenge](https://github.com/DevanshSrajput/Amazon-ML-Challenge)
- ComEM’s candidate pattern assumes blocking has already produced n candidates per record, then a medium LLM matching or comparing step keeps top-k for a stronger selector. That is “cheap model, then expensive model,” with both models being LLMs. The F1 effects are the +16.02% of selecting over pairwise matching, and up to +4% F1 for the cascade over selecting alone, not a million-row throughput number. — [ComEM](https://arxiv.org/html/2405.16884v3)
- ChatMatcher’s stated architecture is the classical filter–verify–cluster pipeline: any filtering method, a 4-bit 7B LLM as the verifier, then bipartite clustering. It claims the interaction of filtering and LLM matching is LLM-specific and does not match what ML/DL matchers do. No candidate counts or F1 were available without the paper body. — [ChatMatcher](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0306437926000980)
- The Qwen3 study (2026) says bi-encoders are the architecture used for retrieval and blocking because each record embedding is independent and can be cached, while cross-encoders remain more accurate as the matcher. No million-row recall figure was extracted. — [arXiv:2607.24688](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2607.24688)
- Ditto’s large-scale number is matching quality, not a blocking recipe: F1 96.5% on company datasets of 789K and 412K records. — [Ditto VLDBJ](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00778-023-00779-z)
- MatchGPT and AnyMatch both warn that hosted LLM matchers are too expensive to run on full candidate sets: MatchGPT down-samples every test set to about 1,250 pairs; AnyMatch cites throughput under 1 KB/s and “5 USD for 5 MB” as the reason a 124M model is the practical zero-shot matcher. Those papers do not themselves demonstrate a multi-index blocker at million-row scale. — [MatchGPT](https://arxiv.org/html/2310.11244v4); [AnyMatch](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.04073v2)

### Inferences
- A reader can sketch the scalable pipeline these sources jointly support, without inventing a stacker: normalize names and addresses; build several exact or hashed indexes (name tokens or name prefixes, address or postcode tokens, and stable identifiers such as phone or house number when they exist — phone and house number are a natural extension of Splink’s postcode/premises rules, but a phone-specific index was not in the Splink examples read); take the union; drop or rank to a small K (the challenge uses K=10; DeepBlocker examples use K=5, 50, or 150); score the survivors with one model (Fellegi–Sunter, LightGBM on string features, a cross-encoder, or a 7B/4B generative matcher); apply a precision-oriented threshold and a one-to-one or clustering cleanup. Embedding ANN (DeepBlocker, FAISS HNSW in Zeakis et al., a Qwen3 bi-encoder in the 2026 study) can be one more branch of that union. It is not, by itself, the method Splink or Zingg ship as the default.
- The precision-heavy tail of the proposed pipeline matches the challenge’s F0.5 objective, τ = 0.650, and one-to-one constraint, and matches Splink’s use of a match-weight threshold. It does not require an LM.
- Putting a 4B LoRA judge after a first LightGBM, then stacking the judge’s score in a second LightGBM, is stricter than anything measured above. ComEM is the academic version of “cheap model then expensive model,” but the cheap model is a medium LLM and the expensive model’s output is the decision. The challenge is the million-row version of “union of blockers, cap at ~10, one LightGBM, precision-heavy rule,” with no judge.

### Gaps
- No opened source reports recall and candidate-set size for a union of name-token, address-token, phone, house-number, and embedding-ANN indexes on a public million-row business corpus.
- The four blocking strategies in the 2026 challenge repository were not itemized in the README extract.
- Splink’s behavior at a stated million-record business-name benchmark (runtime, pair count, F1) was not found; the 26-billion-to-146,000 example is a tutorial, not that benchmark.
- Zingg’s 0.05–1% claim is vendor documentation, not an independent audit.

## How does the proposed cascade compare with what these sources recommend?

### Takeaway
The proposed cascade is more elaborate than any single system in this pass. Its blocking stage and its precision-heavy exit agree with Splink, Zingg, and the 2026 million-row challenge. Its middle — a first LightGBM, then a ~4B LoRA judge, then a second LightGBM that treats the judge score as a feature — was not reported, and was not shown to beat the judge alone or the first tree alone. The literature would either stop after the first tree, or let the 4B model be the final classifier after a cheap candidate cap, or use the 4B model only to label data for the tree.

### Cited Findings
- Proposed stage “bi-encoder blocking”: supported as one branch, not as the whole blocker. Bi-encoders are the caching-friendly blocker in the 2026 Qwen3 study; DeepBlocker and Zeakis et al. use embedding top-K / HNSW; Splink and Zingg use unions of exact and hashed field rules and do not require a bi-encoder. On noisy addresses, Splink’s maintainers warn that correlated address fields, not missing semantics, are what inflate match probabilities. — [arXiv:2607.24688](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2607.24688); [DeepBlocker](https://github.com/qcri/DeepBlocker); [Zeakis et al.](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00778-024-00879-4); [Splink discussion 1975](https://github.com/moj-analytical-services/splink/discussions/1975)
- Proposed stage “LightGBM on string and rival features”: supported as a complete matcher on structured and identifier-heavy sets, not as a proven pre-filter for an LM. In the 2026 tournament it wins 3 of 6 benchmarks under a 10K-label cap, including DBLP-Scholar (Pair-F1 0.541 vs DeepMatcher 0.160) and loses 3 of 6, including Cora (0.891 vs 0.968). The million-row challenge uses exactly one LightGBM after a top-10 union and reports macro F0.5 0.8899. Neither source adds an LM score into that tree. — [arXiv:2607.26298v1](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2607.26298v1); [Amazon-ML-Challenge](https://github.com/DevanshSrajput/Amazon-ML-Challenge)
- Proposed stage “LoRA pairwise judge around 4B”: size and license line up with Qwen3-4B / Qwen3-4B-Instruct-2507 (Apache-2.0, 4.0B), which the 2026 factorial study fine-tunes as a cross-encoder and as a generative matcher. That study says the cross-encoder still beats the bi-encoder at equal size, and that a generative head is not uniformly better. It does not say the 4B score should be a feature. LoRA specifically is unmeasured in that paper’s extract. Llama-3.1-8B is the model with the published ER fine-tune deltas (+17.31 F1 average; WDC 53.36 → 69.19) but it is over the spirit of an Apache/MIT constraint if the Community License citation holds, and 8B is the cap rather than ~4B. AnyMatch’s actual small-model winner is GPT-2 at 124M, mean transfer F1 81.96 versus GPT-4 at 86.36, with no LoRA. — [Qwen3-4B-Instruct-2507](https://huggingface.co/Qwen/Qwen3-4B-Instruct-2507); [arXiv:2607.24688](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2607.24688); [Fine-tuning paper](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.08185v1); [AnyMatch](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.04073v2)
- Proposed stage “judge score is a feature in a second LightGBM”: unmeasured. No opened paper reports this stack, so no F1 lift over the judge or over the first tree can be cited. See the stacker section.
- Proposed stage “precision-heavy decision rule”: supported in form, not as a second learned model. The challenge optimizes macro F0.5, sets τ = 0.650, and uses a near one-to-one constraint (micro precision 0.9718, micro recall 0.8013). ChatMatcher and LLM-CER put a clusterer after the LLM instead of a second tree. Splink thresholds a match weight. — [Amazon-ML-Challenge](https://github.com/DevanshSrajput/Amazon-ML-Challenge); [ChatMatcher](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0306437926000980); [LLM-CER](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.02509)
- Training the 4B judge on the first model’s mistakes is the one interaction the papers do measure, on Llama-3.1-8B rather than on a 4B Qwen: in-domain WDC F1 74.37 (+5.18 versus ordinary fine-tuning) and cross-domain losses of 12.53 and 12.08 F1 versus that ordinary fine-tune. Filtering the training set down to pairs a stronger model already labels correctly drops GPT-4o-mini from a fine-tuned 83.31-class score to 77.06, beside a 77.44 zero-shot. — [Fine-tuning paper, Table 5](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.08185v1)

### Inferences
- If the goal is a sketch a reader can implement from published systems, two simpler pipelines have evidence, and the full stacker does not.
- Pipeline A, million-row and precision-heavy, from Splink/Zingg plus the 2026 challenge: multi-key union (name, address/postcode, other stable fields) → cap near 10 candidates → one LightGBM on string features → threshold tuned for F0.5 and a one-to-one constraint. Reported operating point: 9.65 candidates per record, macro F0.5 0.8899, micro precision 0.9718. No LM. Address/postcode weights must not be treated as independent (Splink discussion 1975).
- Pipeline B, from ComEM, AnyMatch, Steiner, and the Qwen3 study: same candidate cap → one language-model classifier. For zero-shot transfer with a tiny model, GPT-2 124M mean F1 81.96 versus GPT-4 86.36 on academic sets. For in-domain fine-tuning under Apache-2.0 and about 4B, Qwen3-4B is the licensed model the 2026 study actually varies, but its F1 versus a RoBERTa cross-encoder was not extracted. ### Gaps
- No end-to-end F1, precision, or cost figure was found for bi-encoder blocking plus LightGBM plus a ~4B LoRA judge plus a second LightGBM on noisy business names and addresses at million-record scale.
- Whether Qwen3-4B cross-encoder F1 exceeds, ties, or loses to RoBERTa/Ditto on WDC or on address data is unmeasured in the extracts (the factorial paper states a qualitative cross-encoder advantage and does not give the cell values here).
- Rival features (features that compare a candidate against other candidates for the same record) were not a named design in the papers read. The challenge’s one-to-one constraint is the closest decision-time analogue, and it is a rule, not a feature.
